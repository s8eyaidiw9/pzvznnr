# The 20 Best Movies to Watch on Netflix Right Now

There are so many movies on Netflix, it becomes easy to let the service’s homepage guide you to whatever origins or random licenses the algorithm thinks you might enjoy (or to just re-watch every episode of Unbreakable Kimmy Schmidt again). At the same time, Netflix isn’t always a reliable source of great movies from throughout the history of the medium, especially if you’re interested in movies outside of the past five years. This list of the twenty best movies currently streaming on the site isn’t all older stuff; there are several of extremely recent vintage (and not enough from before the 1990s, because that’s a major Netflix blindspot at the moment). But it also represents the cream of the crop (at least as far as mostly-American movies go)—all 20 of these movies are flat-out great, not just half-watchable while you scroll through Twitter. If you’ve missed any of them, check them off your list before they disappear.

Sleeping with Other People

Netflix got a lot of attention last year for helping to rescue the romantic comedy, and its extended streaming of Sleeping with Other People should be good for additional credit. It’s not a Netflix Original; it came out theatrically (however briefly) in 2015. But it’s the best pure romantic comedy of the past decade, at least, a funny and sexy riff on a man and a woman trying to be friends despite their growing mutual attraction. Jason Sudeikis and Alison Brie operate at peak charm levels, and writer-director Leslye Headland (who also co-created the Netflix series Russian Doll) doesn’t contrive elaborate secrets or dopey contrivances to keep her leads apart. Instead, she sustains romantic tension with snappy dialogue and lovely filmmaking. This deserves to be considered a classic of the genre.

Green Room

Jeremy Saulnier has made his name on relentless but ultimately humanity-infused thrillers, usually about regular folks finding themselves in brutally violent situations. His best is this semi-horror picture about a hardcore punk band who find themselves in, yes, a brutally violent situation: After a booking mistake leads to them witnessing a murder, they must fight their way out of a neo-Nazi club. The late Anton Yelchin gives a terrific performance as the band’s frontman and Patrick Stewart is terrifying as the head bad guy.

The Terminator

Before he took a decade or more to repeatedly make the most expensive movie ever made, James Cameron put himself on the map with this lean, mean sci-fi thriller, with Arnold Schwarzenegger as an unstoppable killing machine who has arrived from the future to kill resilient Sarah Connor (Linda Hamilton). Not to be confused with another current Netflix title, Terminator: Salvation—one of the many non-Cameron-directed Terminator movies made since 1991 that aren’t worth much of a damn.

The Magnificent Ambersons

Did Netflix license this Orson Welles movie, famously compromised and cut down from his original vision, because of its superficial similarities to their original film Roma, or to capitalize on interest on their lost Welles film The Other Side of the Wind, the completion of which the company recently funded? Both seem plausible, especially considering how few movies made before 1980 are currently available on the service. Like Roma, Ambersons is a gorgeously made black-and-white movie about a complicated family, although they skew more upper-class than the middle-class folks of the Alfonso Cuaron film. It creates the impression that the full Welles cut would be astounding, but it’s still pretty excellent on its own. Ambersons

recently received the Criterion Blu-ray treatment, but if you’re not yet a Welles convert, you can try it out here first.

A Serious Man

Look, fellow Coen Brothers film No Country for Old Men is also streaming on Netflix, and it’s arguably even better than A Serious Man. For that matter, Netflix also produced the brothers’ most recent film, The Ballad of Buster Scruggs, and it’s excellent. But one of their lesser-seen efforts is also a top-five Coens movie, this darkly funny and fatalistic look at the mounting everyday cruelties endured by a mild-mannered fellow (Michael Stuhlbarg) just trying to be a decent husband and father in the 1960s Midwest. Check it out so you can weigh in on whether the movie is cruel, or just an accurate depiction of how life goes sometimes.

The Truman Show

For the first chunk of his career, Jim Carrey seemed to be the type of hyperactive comic who was always, incurably “on.” Some of his best roles explore that very quality, from the immersive, insane performance art of Andy Kaufman to the more subtly media-trained Truman Burbank, unwitting star of The Truman Show. At the time, Carrey’s performance as Truman was lauded for its restraint, and it does show he’s capable of more than rubber-faced buffoonery that Tommy Lee Jones would famously refuse to sanction. But Peter Weir’s film also teases out the sense that Truman has been affected by starring in a 24/7 reality show, filtering his regular-guy soulfulness through some canned catchphrasing and sitcommy gestures. Carrey is terrific, and this funny, eerie, concise movie was prescient about the coming tidal wave of widely broadcasted real-yet-fake banalities.

Her

Do you ever feel like Siri or Alexa or, for that matter, Netflix really gets you? Filmmaker and music video auteur Spike Jonze takes that notion to a surprisingly affecting extreme in this near-future sci-fi love story about a lonely man (Joaquin Phoenix) who begins a relationship with his computer’s advanced new operating system (voiced by a remarkably expressive Scarlett Johansson). It sounds like a sketch-comedy joke, and while Her has some very funny moments, it’s also a powerful expression of digital-age melancholy.

The Departed

Because it won a Best Picture Oscar and a lot of bros seem to like it, The Departed may have developed a reputation as second-tier Martin Scorsese—gangster revivalism for the casual fan. But this is a tough, bruising, and relentlessly entertaining cops-and-robbers-playing-robber-and-cops remake of Infernal Affairs with its own Bostonian flavor. Just be glad a kickass movie won Best Picture for once! The real Oscar puzzlement is how Leonardo DiCaprio gave one of his best performances ever as a frazzled deep-undercover cop, yet scored his Best Actor nomination that same year for the far inferior Blood Diamond.

Cool Hand Luke

One of Paul Newman’s best casts him as a rebellious prisoner chafing against authority (and eating a crap-ton of eggs). In a way, it’s an engaging prison-break movie without much actual escape, somewhere between the square-jawed tenacity of The Great Escape and the authority-flouting spirit of One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest. Newman’s easy (and quietly layered) charm is put to expert use, and George Kennedy won an Oscar for his role as a fellow prisoner.

Silence of the Lambs

Horror movies rarely get many Oscar nominations, let alone wins, and when a horror movie like Silence of the Lambs, say, sweeps five big categories (picture, director, actor, actress, screenplay), it’s almost immediately recategorized as a classier-sounding “thriller.” But what is Hannibal Lecter (Anthony Hopkins in his defining role) if not a fascinating monster, what is this serial-killer hunt if not terrifying, and what is Silence of the Lambs if not a horror movie? Hopkins gets all the famous lines, but Jodie Foster’s steeliness and vulnerability carries the movie home.

Annie Hall

Here’s a tricky one: It makes sense that Woody Allen has become persona non grata in Hollywood after his daughter’s abuse allegations resurfaced. If your stomach doesn’t automatically turn at the sight of him, Annie Hall is one of the great romantic comedies ever made, funny and freewheeling and bittersweet, with an all-timer of a Diane Keaton performance. Allen was always something of an acquired taste, and that’s even truer now. But if you want to watch one of his movies to see what his whole deal was, this is probably the one to check out.

Monty Python’s Life of Brian

The British comedy troupe Monty Python takes on the Biblical epic with this satirical travestying of how people and religion can warp each other, featuring a non-savior named Brian (Graham Chapman) born on the same day as Jesus Christ, and subsequently mistaken for the Messiah. Monty Python and the Holy Grail is also streaming on Netflix, and it’s the more consistently hilarious of the two; indeed, it’s one of the funniest movies ever made. But Life of Brian may be the most ambitious and impressive of Python projects. You won’t likely see a funnier movie with an extended crucifixion scene.

Heathers

This teen-movie classic remains a high point of its subgenre—or maybe the only movie of note in its own sub-subgenre of pitch-black comedy featuring teenage murders mistaken for suicide. It’s certainly a movie that’s so itself that it’s difficult to picture actors other than Winona Ryder and Christian Slater pulling off their roles: She as a conflicted clique member, he as a charismatic loose cannon who wants to kill the in-crowd. Heathers and its witless ripoff Jawbreaker both celebrate prominent anniversaries this year. Far be it from us to tell you which cult movie is worthy of your adulation but, on the other hand: Accept no substitutes.

Heat

The Netflix-funded The Irishman (due out in fall 2019) is the fourth movie to feature both Robert De Niro and Al Pacino. The first is a great movie where they share no scenes (The Godfather Part II). The third is a lousy movie where they share many scenes (Righteous Kill). The second is Heat, and gets it just right: Pacino plays a cop (“on the edge,” naturally) chasing a gruff career criminal (De Niro, naturally), and they square off face-to-face in exactly one scene, where writer-director Michael Mann sits them down in a diner booth for coffee and a chat. Even in that scene, Mann separates his stars, cutting back and forth between single shots, rather than constantly placing them in the frame together. Their interactions aren’t directly physical, but play out on each other’s faces, in each other’s eyes. It’s a perfect scene. The rest of the movie is terrific, too, with expertly staged bank robberies, chases, and detective work—and a sprawling cast that goes beyond the dueling legends at its center.

Zodiac

David Fincher’s Seven made more money, but his real serial-killer masterpiece is this slow-burning but intoxicating chronicle of the real-life Zodiac Killer, who was never caught. Mark Ruffalo, Jake Gyllenhaal and Robert Downey Jr. play a cop, a cartoonist/amateur sleuth, and a journalist, respectively, all drawn into the Bay Area mystery. Though its murder scenes are terrifying, this is really a movie about the perils of poor record-keeping and inadequate information-sharing (and, thanks to the excellent performances and Fincher’s obsessive craft, far more fascinating than that may sound).

Kicking and Screaming

Yet another great filmmaker adopted by Netflix, Noah Baumbach had his wonderful film The Meyerowitz Stories picked up by the streaming service in 2017, with another film (starring Adam Driver and Scarlett Johansson) in the pipeline for 2019. Kicking and Screaming (not to be confused with the Will Ferrell soccer comedy) is a less starry affair; just as it was during its 1995 release, the most recognizable faces in it are Eric Stoltz and Parker Posey. But the Early Baumbach regulars who make up the rest of the cast—Josh Hamilton, Carlos Jacott, Chris Eigeman—are all hilarious as overthinking, ill-prepared college graduates fumbling their way through that awkward first year out (made more awkward by their decision to continue living in their old college town). This is one of the smartest movies ever made about that brand of middle-class purgatory, and it is endlessly, brilliantly quotable. Watch it immediately, so the next day you can claim to be reminiscing about it already.

The Graduate

Hey, speaking of postgraduate ennui: Dustin Hoffman and Mike Nichols essentially defined it onscreen with this 1967 comedy about a college grad (Hoffman, playing way younger than his actual age) who drifts into a summertime affair with Mrs. Robinson (Anne Bancroft), an associate of his parents. The Graduate is both very much of its late-60s time (complete with a Simon & Garfunkel soundtrack!) and impressively relevant today; its laughs have real sting—as does its happy-but-maybe-not ending, one of the best in American cinema.

Schindler’s List

This spot could be taken by several other Spielberg classics currently streaming on Netflix; frankly, if we weren’t trying to balance out this list with a variety of filmmakers, he could have almost half of it with the likes of Jaws, Lincoln, and a bunch of Indiana Jones pictures. So yes, if you haven’t seen Jaws or Raiders of the Lost Ark, by all means, watch them on Netflix (though between repertory screenings, midnight showings, and Fathom Events, those are two older movies that are probably easier than most to catch on a big screen if you hold out). But if you’ve been putting off Schindler’s List, and you missed the recent 25th anniversary re-release, it’s one of his best: a disciplined, heartbreaking Holocaust drama about an opaque businessman (Liam Neeson) who decides to do something good in the midst of unspeakable horrors.

Pulp Fiction

Quentin Tarantino’s kinetic, funny, harrowing triptych of Los Angeles crime stories turns 25 this fall, and even two and a half decades of knockoffs haven’t flattened its fizz. At the time, it represented a major comeback for John Travolta; now, so many more comebacks and missteps and comebacks and Gottis later, it feels like a metaphor for the awe-inspiring ups and downs of his career. In his big segment with Uma Thurman, he narrowly escapes a terrible fate—only to stumble into one later, and then reappear thanks to the movie’s shuffled chronology. Classic Travolta! You can kill him but he’ll come right back! If that feels like a superficial, star-centric reading, well, movies are almost foremost on Tarantino’s mind.

Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind

Director Michel Gondry has made some of the best music videos of all time—and one of the best movies, too. His videos utilize mind-bending perspective shifts, handmade-looking practical effects, and a melancholic whimsy to create woozy, often playful spaces for pop songs. It makes sense, then, that his best feature film takes place largely in the vast expanse of the mind and its memories, as Joel (Jim Carrey) enlists a strange company to eliminate painful memories of his ex-girlfriend Clementine (Kate Winslet), who he learns has undergone the same procedure. The movie refracts moments from their relationship through the distortion of memory, as Joel scrambles to escape his own impulsive decision. Carrey and Winslet give tour de force performances, and so does Gondry, as the movie coheres many of his stylistic touchstones into a devastating (and sometimes uncomfortable relatable) treatise on compatibility, loneliness, and whether we’re doomed to repeat our past mistakes.
