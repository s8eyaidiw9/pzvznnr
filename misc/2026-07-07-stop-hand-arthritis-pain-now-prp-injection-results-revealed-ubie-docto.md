What Is PRP and How Might It Help Hand Arthritis?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is a concentration of your own blood platelets in a small volume of plasma. These platelets release growth factors that may:

Reduce inflammation

Promote tissue repair

Modulate pain signals

For hand osteoarthritis, PRP is injected directly into the affected joints or tendon sheaths to target pain and improve function.

How PRP Injections Are Performed

Blood Draw• A small amount (20–60 mL) of your blood is drawn.

Centrifugation• The blood is spun to separate platelets from red and white cells.

Preparation• The platelet layer is collected and mixed with a tiny amount of plasma.

Injection• Under ultrasound guidance, the clinician injects PRP into the arthritic joint(s).

Most sessions take 30–45 minutes from start to finish.

What the Research Says: PRP for Hand Pain Reviews

While PRP is more widely studied in knees and elbows, emerging data for hand arthritis is promising:

A 2020 trial in the Journal of Hand Surgery showed a 30–50% reduction in pain scores at 6 months post-PRP compared to baseline.

A pilot study published in PM&R (2019) reported improved grip strength and patient satisfaction in over 70% of participants.

In a 2021 systematic review of small hand OA trials, PRP outperformed placebo and matched or exceeded corticosteroid injections in longer-term pain relief (3–12 months).

Key takeaway: PRP for hand pain reviews generally report moderate to significant symptom relief, with many patients enjoying better hand function for up to a year.

Benefits and Limitations

Benefits

Uses your own blood—very low risk of allergic reaction

Minimal downtime; most return to normal activity in 24–48 hours

Potentially disease-modifying effects (not just pain masking)

Limitations

Not a guaranteed cure; about 15–20% may see minimal change

Cost can range from $500 to $1,500 per injection, often self-paid

Multiple sessions (2–4) may be needed for full effect

Long-term data (beyond 12–18 months) is still emerging

Who Is a Good Candidate?

Consider PRP if you:

Have mild to moderate hand osteoarthritis confirmed by X-ray

Have tried conventional measures (NSAIDs, therapy, splints) with limited success

Are willing to self-pay or verify insurance coverage

Seek to delay or avoid surgery

PRP may be less suitable if you:

Have severe deformity needing surgical correction

Have an active infection or bleeding disorder

Take certain blood-thinning medications (consult your doctor)

What to Expect: Timeline & Aftercare

First Week• Mild swelling or aching is normal.• Ice and over-the-counter pain relief may help.

Weeks 2–6• Gradual reduction in pain.• Begin gentle hand therapy exercises.

Months 2–6• Peak benefit usually occurs by 3 months.• Many patients report 6–12 months of relief.

Maintenance• Some choose a "booster" injection at 6–12 months if relief wanes.

Practical Considerations

Cost & Coverage: PRP is often considered experimental by insurers. Expect out-of-pocket fees unless part of a clinical trial.

Number of Injections: Most protocols recommend 2–3 injections spaced 2–6 weeks apart.
