# Shannon Sharpe Reveals New Details About His Fight With Prostate Cancer

[Reveals New](https://nootropic-atlas.escortskhi.com/)

In September, Shannon Sharpe rewrote his history with one revelation.

The verbose co-host of FOX Sports’ Skip & Shannon: Undisputed bared his truth on Twitter, FOX NFL Sunday, Instagram, and People about being diagnosed with prostate cancer in 2016. The 54-year-old Pro Football Hall-of-Famer with a gladiatorial physique that still looks NFL-ready spoke candidly about keeping his diagnosis from his children and Undisputed colleagues, his family’s history with cancer making him reckon with his mortality, and being cancer-free since 2019. But the whole story wasn't told.

While Sharpe was entertaining (and infuriating) millions on Undisputed with his “bull jive” criticisms of athletes and cognac-inspired soliloquies on LeBron's greatness, he was being subjected to blood tests every three months for 18 months after his 2016 diagnosis. In 2017, the same year he called out Dallas Cowboys owner Jerry Jones for threatening players, he underwent prostate seed implantation surgery “where they actually implant radioactive seeds directly into the prostate,” according to his conversation with Men’s Health.

Watching his father lose his life to lung cancer at a young age played a significant role in his screening for cancer since retiring from the NFL in 2004. But, he's seldom discussed the struggle he witnessed his father endure, which has remained indelible in his mind decades later. "I watched my daddy have to have both of his jaw bones removed, part of his throat removed, and all this stuff. But, he was too far gone," Sharpe revealed. "So for me, I was like, ‘Nah, I don't want it to be too late.’"

Now, athletes and entertainers he's certain he never gave his number to have been blowing up his phone to thank him for sharing a journey they've been suffering with in silence. In a chat with Men's Health, he dove a bit deeper into the unseen struggles and triumphs of opening up about his cancer journey.

Men's Health: You got your diagnosis in 2016. How long did it take you before you became cancer free?

I had to get blood tests every three months. I did that for 18 months; then it went to twice a month until my PSA (Prostate-Specific Antigen) levels dropped to 0.0. That was about a year and a half, almost two years.

What surgery did you have?

The cancer never spread; it never got outside the prostate. So I had a specific surgery, prostate seed implant, where they actually implant radioactive seed directly into the prostate.

Did you have any hesitation or reservations about doing the surgery?

No. I wanted to know how soon I could have the surgery. How soon will I be back to being Shannon and not have to worry about this? That was my only concern. The doctors I met felt this was my best course of action. It was very centralized in one area. It did not spread.

You didn't tell your kids about your diagnosis when you found out. When did you finally reveal it to them?

No, my kids didn't know until after the surgery, until after I was done. Once I was cancer free, I told them one by one, “Hey, I went through something; your dad had cancer.” They were like, “Dad, are you going to be okay?” I was like, “Your dad is fine. I've already gone through the process, and I'm done with it.” I didn't want anybody else to tell my story. I didn't want something to leak. I know I felt the most comfortable telling these four people. As the process started playing out more, I felt comfortable telling people.

How hard was it not to tell your children?

I was just enjoying that time, Keith. Once I had the surgery, I enjoyed their birthdays. Their birthdays were coming and going. They never knew anything was wrong because I was the same dad. I interacted with them the same way. I was still outgoing. I was still telling jokes. Nothing really changed. So there was no reason for them to suspect anything. Because we caught this so early, the doctors had assured me that I would be fine. My mind never thought of the worst situation outside of that. The one thing I was concerned with was, “How soon am I going to be fine?” The only thing I was concerned about was how soon I was going to be a hundred percent cancer-free.

You mentioned you had a history where people in your family didn’t get the medical attention they needed until it was too late. Is there a specific memory you could draw from?

Growing up, you had to be near or on your deathbed before you went to the doctor. You weren't just going to the doctor for routine things. If you had a toothache, you had to get your tooth pulled. It wasn't like you were going to a dentist. You had to have an open wound that required major stitches before you went to the doctor. Growing up like that, we get into a state where we think, “Well, I don't see blood. I can still do this; I can still do that. So it can't be anything that major.” I watched my daddy have to have both of his jaw bones removed, part of his throat removed, and all this stuff. But, it was too far gone. And to see my uncles go through what they went through, it was too late. So for me, I was like, “Nah, I don't want it to be too late,” Especially knowing what I know; maybe they didn't know, perhaps they did, perhaps they were afraid, maybe they were scared. Maybe they didn't have the medical coverage they needed and didn't want to be a burden. That’s also a big thing: We don't want to burden our loved ones with medical bills. And if something were to happen to us, the family still has to take care of that. That was a big thing in my family; we didn't want to burden those left behind to take care of the things we created.

Why else do you think there's a stigma against going to the doctor in the black community?

You hear things. I mean, the same thing happened with Covid. You hear about the research and think, “Are they just guinea-pigging me? I'm not getting any better.” Also, there are enormous medical bills—all those things. I came from a situation where we didn't have a whole lot. A day at the hospital is a month’s worth of work. Who wants to do that? So my family grew up like, “Nah, it's not even worth it. It's not worth you being in debt for the rest of your life. It's just better for me to just go home.” That's the way my family thought.

But, especially with the prostate, it's not as invasive now. You can do the blood test and things of that nature. But we've never been exposed to going to the doctors, especially people my age. Now I think young black men are more willing to go to the doctor. But not when I was a kid. Nobody went to the doctor because they weren't feeling well.

How long after being cancer free did Janssen approach you for Talk That Talk?

I think we started talking at the beginning of this year. I was content with not sharing. I had only told a tiny group of people—my closest family and friends. When Janssen approached me, they had no idea I had been diagnosed and treated for prostate cancer. They didn't know this until we were shooting the spot, and I sat down to discuss it.

What is the importance of Talk That Talk?

Man, it's been unbelievable. When I went on [FOX NFL Sunday], many people reached out to me, some famous, some not famous. They told me, “Man, I'm glad you shared it because I went through something very similar. I don't feel bad now. I don't feel alone. I'm glad you can use your platform to tell other Black men like myself, and men in general, to go out there and get screened early because it could save your life, and you're right.” I think it helped that I'm not only a spokesperson, but I've gone through what I'm telling them.

Who has reached out to you since your cancer diagnosis revelation?

Athletes and entertainers have reached out to me. I see common people walking the street, and they pull me aside to say, "Thank you for sharing your story." Many of these diseases don't care what lifestyle you live (laughs). I have a history of cancer in my family. I joke with my mom and say, “Hey, you had the idea I'd be perfect. I mean, big, strong athletic, smart. But, you put the two worst genetics together, as far as health, and you see what you guys created?” Sometimes your lifestyle plays a role in how healthy you are. But in this situation, I had an ideal lifestyle. But cancer doesn't care anything about that. It doesn't care if you're rich, poor, black, white, big, tall, small, or short. It didn't care about anything like that. But, for me, it was getting the screening, knowing my history, and then whatever happened after, I would rather know than not know. I would rather be safe than sorry.

Since you’ve had a family history of medical issues, how have you been speaking with your children about their health going forward and educating them?

My son is about to be a father, and he's said he would start getting screenings in his mid-thirties, just like I did. I've only had one uncle on my mom's side reach 70. He's 72. So most of my uncles died in their fifties. I think the oldest one reached about the age of 64 but had a couple die early, around 61. One died at 56. So knowing the history on both sides of my family, I take my health very, very, very, very seriously. People can say I’m a hypochondriac. I’ll be a hypochondriac at the doctor's office (laughs). I'm very fortunate that God has blessed me with a great job and medical coverage. I'm able to go to the doctor. We know the inequities that happen in the healthcare system. But, I wish everybody had the type of healthcare they could just go to and not even worry about.

Any future advocacy plans for this subject?

Hopefully, we'll continue to work together. Hopefully, they've been very pleased with the responses and the positive feedback they've received. It's been something that I'm getting a lot of attention from this. I don't think I've done anything special. I've just tried to enlighten people and raise awareness for this disease, saying, “Okay, yeah, there's a chance that you might get this disease, but if you get screened earlier, you get it detected earlier, there's a great chance for survival.” So I've just played a very, very small role. Janssen has given me a platform to spread the message and get the word out. So hopefully, they've been pleased with it. It seems like a great partnership. So hopefully, in the future, we will continue to work together and get the message out, the word out, and we can continue to save lives.
