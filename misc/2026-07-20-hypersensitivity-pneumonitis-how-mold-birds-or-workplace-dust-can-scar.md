Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis: How Mold, Birds, or Workplace Dust Can Scar Your Lungs Over Time

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is an immune-driven lung disease triggered by inhaling mold spores, bird proteins, or workplace dusts. Repeated exposure causes chronic inflammation in the alveoli, eventually leading to lung scarring (fibrosis) and impaired breathing.

Key facts about hypersensitivity pneumonitis:

Early symptoms: Fever, cough, and shortness of breath—often mistaken for the flu

Later symptoms: Persistent cough, exercise intolerance, and fatigue

Common triggers: Moldy environments, pet birds, agricultural or industrial dust

Why early detection matters: Removing the trigger early can prevent permanent lung damage

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is an inflammatory lung condition that develops when your immune system overreacts to inhaled particles. Over weeks, months or even years of repeated exposure to certain allergens—like mold spores, bird proteins or occupational dust—your tiny air sacs (alveoli) can become damaged and scarred. Left unmanaged, this scarring (fibrosis) can lead to persistent breathing problems and reduced lung function.

What Is Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis?

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis is sometimes called "extrinsic allergic alveolitis." It's not an allergy in the usual sense (like hay fever), but rather a complex immune response in the lungs. Key points:

Immune cells attack inhaled particles that reach the alveoli.

Chronic inflammation can thicken and scar lung tissue.

Recognizing the Symptoms

Early detection of hypersensitivity pneumonitis can slow or stop progression. Symptoms vary by stage:

Acute (hours after heavy exposure)

Fever, chills, general malaise

Dry cough, chest tightness

Shortness of breath

Subacute (days to weeks of moderate exposure)

Gradual onset of cough and breathlessness

Fatigue, unintended weight loss

Mild chest discomfort

Chronic (months to years of low-level exposure)

Persistent cough and progressive breathlessness

Clubbing (widening) of finger tips

Crackling sounds ("Velcro crackles") when listening to the lungs

Because symptoms overlap with asthma, COPD or infections, HP is often misdiagnosed. If you notice recurring breathing symptoms tied to certain environments, consider speaking with a specialist.

Diagnosis: Piecing Together the Puzzle

Diagnosing hypersensitivity pneumonitis usually involves:

Detailed History– Questions about home, work, hobbies, birds and mold exposure.

Physical Exam– Listening for crackles, checking for clubbing of nails.

Pulmonary Function Tests– Measure lung capacity and how well you move air in and out.

Imaging– High-resolution CT (computed tomography) scans show inflammation and scarring patterns.

Blood Tests– Detect specific antibodies (precipitins) against known allergens.

Bronchoscopy with Biopsy (sometimes)– Direct sampling of lung tissue or fluid to confirm inflammation type.

A collaborative approach—often involving a pulmonologist, allergist, and occupational medicine specialist—yields the most accurate diagnosis.

Treatment Strategies

Removal of the Allergen

The most critical step: stop exposure to mold, birds or workplace dust.

Relocation, professional mold remediation, improved ventilation or appropriate protective equipment (respirators) may be needed.

Medications

Corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) to reduce inflammation.

Immunosuppressants (e.g., azathioprine) in chronic cases to slow immune response.

Dosing and duration depend on severity and response.

Supportive Care

Oxygen therapy for low blood oxygen levels.

Pulmonary rehabilitation: supervised exercise and breathing techniques.

Vaccinations (flu, pneumonia) to prevent infections.

Monitoring and Follow-Up

Regular lung function tests and imaging to track progression.

Adjust treatment plans based on response and side effects.

Early intervention can often reverse inflammation and preserve lung function. Once fibrosis is established, treatment focuses on preventing further scarring and optimizing quality of life.

Preventing Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

Maintain good indoor air quality: control humidity below 50%, fix leaks, clean HVAC systems.

Use masks or respirators when handling dusty or moldy materials.

For bird owners: keep cages clean, use proper ventilation and consider professional cleaning services.

Adequate ventilation, dust suppression systems, exposure monitoring.

Personal protective equipment (PPE) and regular health screenings for workers.

Talking to Your Doctor

Discuss your complete exposure history—home mold, pets and work environment. Bring any prior test results and note how symptoms change with location and activity. Effective communication helps your healthcare team:

Identify possible triggers faster

Order the right tests sooner

Tailor treatment to your specific situation

Living Well with Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

With proper management and lifestyle adjustments, many people live full lives:

Stick to your treatment plan and attend follow-up visits.

Incorporate breathing exercises and gentle exercise into daily routines.

Seek support groups or counseling if the diagnosis feels overwhelming.

Monitor indoor air quality and maintain preventive measures.

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis can be serious if left unchecked, but early recognition and focused treatment can minimize lung damage and improve your long-term outlook.

Speak to a doctor about any concerning or life-threatening symptoms. Proper diagnosis and timely intervention are key to protecting your lungs and maintaining your quality of life.
