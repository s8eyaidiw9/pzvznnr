# Scientists Are One Step Closer to Letting Humans 'Breathe' Through Their Butts

[to Letting Humans](https://lumina-dermis.sunsimiaosd.com/blog/4405425654.html)

THIS ISN'T A joke. Though it kind of started out as one.

Scientists are now one step closer to answering a critical question: Is it possible to breathe through your butt?

But first, some background. In 2024, Japanese researchers from Osaka University were awarded the Ig Nobel Prize—a satirical award for weird and fascinating science discoveries—in physiology for their investigation of butt-breathing in animals.

In nature, butt-breathing actually isn’t a new idea. Animals like turtles and certain fish species can breathe through their backend. And the scientists who earned the Ig Nobel Prize found that mammalian assholes, like ones from rodents and pigs, actually can absorb oxygen through the rectum in emergency situations.

And now we know that humans might be capable of doing it, too. Earlier this week, the same Japanese scientists published a new study in Med that reported the results of the first human trial testing the safety of the method they plan to use to deliver oxygen through the backdoor.

You might be thinking that nobody asked for this. But if butt breathing in humans works the same way as it does in animals, the researchers suggest "enteral ventilation" could be a last resort to keep people with severe respiratory failure alive.

The clinical trial included 27 healthy men who volunteered to hold between 25 and 1,500 milliliters of non-oxygenated perfluorocarbon liquid in their rectum for an hour. (Yes, really.)

The results: There were no serious, harmful effects. However, people who took the highest volume of the liquid did complain about abdominal bloating, discomfort, and some pain. Out of the 27, only 7 people couldn’t make it to the hour mark. The highest tolerable dose was at 1,000 milliliters.

"This is the first human data, and the results are limited solely to demonstrating the safety of the procedure and not its effectiveness," says Takanori Takebe, MD, PhD, a leading expert in organoid medicine at the University of Osaka and Cincinnati Children’s and senior study author. "But now that we have established tolerance, the next step will be to evaluate how effective the process is for delivering oxygen to the bloodstream."

The idea is to use a perfluorocarbon liquid containing a highly concentrated version of oxygen, which would permeate through the intestinal walls and into the bloodstream (as the 2024 study indicates could be possible). Takebe and her team would then measure how long it takes for blood oxygen levels to improve.
