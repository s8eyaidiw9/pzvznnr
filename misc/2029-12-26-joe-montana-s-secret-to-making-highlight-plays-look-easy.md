WHEREVER YOU'D RANK Joe Montana among the Hall of Fame quarterbacks—check that, the greatest players—in NFL history, none in the pantheon had a signature moment like The Catch. The play needed no further description, neither the literal play call like Bart Starr running the 31 Wedge in the Ice Bowl game, nor metaphorical dressing up like, say, the Immaculate Reception in Pittsburgh. It was just The Catch, the play against which all clutch plays would be and are still today measured.

For those of us old enough to remember the NFC championship game playing out in real time at Candlestick Park, it’s hard to imagine that The Catch will mark its 40th anniversary on Jan 10. For those without grey hair, it’s impossible to count how many times you’ve watched the sequence play out in an NFL Films feature.

Montana taking the snap third and three at the Dallas six-yard-line. Searching for a target in the end zone but finding none. Then rolling, rolling, rolling right, fading, fading, fading back under pressure from Ed (Too Tall Jones) set to bury him at the sidelines. Lobbing the ball off his wrong foot, seemingly to throw it into the crowd. And then, improbably, impossibly, the lanky Dwight Clark, Montana’s favorite target, soaring high and snagging the ball at its apex, where no defender could even wave at it.

The greatness in this moment wasn’t the quarterback’s scrambling or his off-balance toss—really, the single physical play most extraordinary was Clark’s pulling down the ball. It’s called The Catch, not The Pass, for a reason. No, what Montana demonstrated on that play was what separated him from all other QBs in his era: the ability to think the game. Not simply in theory, not in reading a defense and calling an audible at the line of scrimmage. No, Montana talks about “decision-making ability” but when he describes it he evokes mindfulness.

“As you're dropping back to pass, you can already feel what’s happening,” Montana says, in a recent interview promoting a six-part biographical documentary set to air on Peacock on January 6th. “Is it a blitz? If there’s pressure, where’s it coming from? Do you have protection? Do you need to protect yourself? Then you have to check your options—first option, second, third and keep going through when the play’s getting extended.”

The Catch sent San Francisco to its first Super Bowl and thus introduced the national audience to Joe Montana, then just 25, however preciously poised he seemed. He looked less the part of a NFL quarterback than, say, John Elway and Dan Marino. He was generously listed at 6-feet-2 and 205 pounds—those numbers don’t tell the story so well as his nickname in the locker room, “Chicken Legs.” And he wasn’t particularly charismatic—not the team leader as rock star. But none dealt with pressure better than Montana. His gift was something that the scouting combine can’t measure: He was ever in the moment. The producers were on point when they came up with a title for the documentary series: Cool Under Pressure.

Montana would lead the 49ers to a championship over Cincinnati just two weeks after The Catch and to three more Super Bowl victories. He’d go on to win a couple of league MVPs. Earning the nickname Joe Cool, he established himself as the master of the last-minute comeback. No matter how indelible the memories of Montana, though, he occupied a curious place with the public, perhaps unique in sports: He was the Low-Profile Superstar.

These days many purported experts would want to reduce athletes as the sum of their data. Montana is arguably the best example of a star whose greatest weapon lurked under his helmet, whose hours of hundreds of hours of preparation in the film room separated him from more gifted peers.

“Everybody would think that it was an easy road along the way for me,” he says. “This series shows all the way from high school to Notre Dame and to the NFL things I went through a lot of ups and downs, trials and tribulations, just like everyone else outside the game.”

As a high-schooler in small-town western Pennsylvania, Montana stood out in whatever game he picked up—he’ll duly note in conversation, “a Parade Magazine Third-Team High-School All-American.” Montana’s career predates the advent of recruiting services with their five-star ratings, although given his modest physical stature, he might have been designated as only a four-star prize when he left Monongahela and set off to Notre Dame.

INSTANT SUCCESS WASN'T going to be in the cards in South Bend and Montana knew that going in—the program didn’t allow freshman to play for or even practice with the varsity. Nonetheless, from his first weeks on campus, Montana worried that he might not be able to stick around long enough to show what he could do. “I was so overwhelmed obviously first by football and it just carried over into the classroom,” he says. “Academically, I just felt like I never had enough time. I should have done what some of the guys did—they went to summer school there and picked up credits. That would have put me on a better track. You don’t want a full load of classes that first year.”

As a sophomore, Montana was down the depth chart and spent a few Saturday afternoons entirely on the sidelines. When he was sent into games, it was for mop-up duty and he completed on 28 passes for the season. Nonetheless, he did drop a couple of significant hints that he possessed something out of the ordinary. The first came on the road in Chapel Hill—Montana entered the game with Notre Dame trailing Carolina 14-6 with 5:11 left to play. On consecutive drives that took up only 62 seconds on the game clock, he racked up 129 yards passing, and the Irish scored a pair of touchdowns to pull out a 21-14 win. Then, the next week Montana engineered an even more improbable comeback in South Bend: Notre Dame was trailing Air Force 30-10 when he entered the game, again in the fourth quarter. The Irish escaped with a 31-30 win. The miracles mattered only so much, however, and Notre Dame’s first-year coach Dan Devine didn’t entertain any thought of installing Montana as his starting QB.

What should have been his junior year was washed out by a separated shoulder. The timing did work out in his favor—he suffered the injury in practice before the Irish’s opening game and thus, he was able to redshirt and extend his eligibility. Still, Montana had been on campus for three years and a chance to establish himself as a starter, never mind as a star, seemed more distant than ever. His Parade Magazine honors were looking like they might wind up the highlight of his career.

The contrast to other star athletes was never sharper than at this juncture. Michael Jordan had started as a freshman at North Carolina and had beat the buzzer with a jump shot to win a national title. At the end of his junior year, Jordan led the U.S. team to a gold medal at the 1984 Olympics and later that summer, at age 21, he signed a five-year deal with Nike. You might have guessed that one day they’d build a statue of Jordan or, yes, produce a documentary in tribute to him. With Montana, you couldn’t have been sure that he’d even land on Notre Dame’s alumni mailing list. He seemed far closer to his teammate Daniel Ruettiger (a.k.a., Rudy of later inspirational-bioflick fame) than a pro prospect. You might have even wagered that Montana would transfer to a smaller school just to get a chance to play rather than wait in vain.

When back from his redshirt year, Montana managed to turn his fortunes around—though with little support and apparently no enthusiasm from the cold-fish Devine, the anti-Ted Lasso of this story, the nearest thing that the documentary has to a villain. Montana started the 1977 season as a third-stringer—reasonable or at least defensible, given the small sample Devine had to work with. He didn’t appear in the first two games that season: A win at Pittsburgh and a loss at Ole Miss, the latter dropping Notre Dame out of the Top 10 in the national rankings.

That set the stage for, The Catch notwithstanding, what should be considered as Montana’s watershed moment: Notre Dame was losing Purdue in South Bend and Devine had pulled his struggling starter, Rusty Litsch, and sent in back-up Gary Forystek; when Forystek suffered a calamitous and career-ending hit that broke a vertebra and his clavicle and left him severely concussed, Devine opted not to try Montana but rather gave Litsch another shot. With the Irish down 24-14 with 11 minutes remaining, Devine finally sent in Montana, who sparked a three-touchdown rally and a 31-24 win. The coach had little choice but to make Montana the starting QB and he’d go on a glorious run the rest of the way, winning out, climaxing in the Cotton Bowl where a one-sided upset of top-ranked Texas landed Notre Dame the national championship.

The magic didn’t quite carry over to Montana’s last year in South Bend. As a fifth-year senior, he led the team to a 9-3 season and a return to the Cotton Bowl. Then came yet another moment in Montana lore: It was a perfect storm of challenges, a perfect ice storm that is, with gale-force winds that dropped the wind chill factor to minu-6 Fahrenheit. Trainers pulled a flu-ridden Montana from the game and, in the dressing room, after determining that his body temperature had plunged to 96 degrees, they diagnosed him with hypothermia. The team doctor prescribed him chicken soup and a warm blanket. Nonetheless, with Notre Dame down 34-12, Montana returned to the field in the third quarter, rallying the Irish for a 35-34 victory.

It had taken an abundance of emotional resolve to wait out an opportunity that didn’t look like it was coming at Notre Dame. What became known as “The Chicken Soup Game” was indisputable proof of his physical resilience and that is probably one correction the Peacock documentary will make—Montana will be remembered as a precision passer but was never given the credit he earned for toughness. “There are two things, being hurt and being injured,” he says. “I played hurt. All kinds of things through my career. Flu, passing kidney stones the night before a game, really banged up. That’s playing hurt. You play hurt because everybody gets hurt. If you’re injured, that’s another thing.” I’m pretty sure that a panel of doctors would declare that hypothermia is in the range of “injured” rather than “hurt,” but no matter.

MONTANA WENT INTO the 1979 NFL draft lightly considered. NFL scouts didn’t really put any stock in the college heroics—like Dan Devine, they didn’t see it even though it was out there in plain sight on college football’s biggest stages. Montana just didn’t fit the mold. It wasn’t a strong year for quarterbacks—only three were selected in the first round (Phil Simms whom you know, Jack Thompson and Steve Fuller whom you almost certainly don’t). On the recommendation of 49ers coach Bill Walsh who did see it, San Francisco wound up taking Montana with the last pick of the third round. Some figured that he might have still been available a couple of rounds later.

In retrospect, many fans and media have given Walsh the lion’s share of the credit for Montana’s own successes, claiming that the quarterback happened to be in the right place at the right time, that is, under center with Walsh’s West Coast Offense his to execute. Giving the coach his due, his innovative system opened up of the previously ground-bound game and ushered in the NFL’s modern era. Yet after The Catch and on the eve of the 49ers’ first Super Bowl, Walsh made it clear no one should have mistaken Montana for a passenger when in fact he was the catalyst. He put it down to his quarterback’s personality. ''People will play for him more smoothly than someone like (Joe) Namath who's flamboyant and attracts a lot of attention,'' he said. ''Joe does not have the bravado that certain people who are less smart have.''

Montana’s work ethic also endeared him to Walsh, whose almost professorial mien was cover for a tough taskmaster behind the scenes. “We had 130 [pass patterns] in every game plan out of different formations,” Montana says. “I knew exactly what I was looking for. I screened hours of film every week—they were big canisters back then. Some guys weren’t willing to put in the time and the work—they wouldn’t even open the canisters. Bill knew who wasn’t doing the work. He’d leave a $100 bill in a canister and it would still be there when it was handed back to him. Bill would test me, every way possible, not just that I watched the film but that I understood it. And the thing was, I wanted to do that work. I understood that it made you a better player, especially at quarterback. It’s really how I approached everything.”

Doubters would claim that Montana lacked the arm strength of the legendary quarterbacks, that he didn’t go deep and opted for a possession game of slants and quick hitters—a conventional wisdom that formed early in his career that stuck with him through four Super Bowl victories. Montana maintains that was a function of surrounding talent. “Starting out, we had Freddy Solomon and Dwight,” he says. “Both guys were pretty fast, but they're not Jerry Rice or John Taylor. When Jerry and John came in, then we looked to throw the ball down the field.”

The Peacock documentary promises to be a compelling look at Montana, but it comes very late in the day—somehow achieving a symmetry with the signature moments of the Hall of Famer’s career. If it had come along nearer to his playing days, then perhaps cliches about athletic immortality would have been kicked around. But now, 40 years after The Catch, athletic mortality is a not-so-subtle subtext. So many of those famous names are no longer with us: Dwight Clark died in 2018; Freddie Solomon, the other 49ers wideout in those early days, passed away some years before that; and likewise, three offensive linemen who threw key blocks while Montana looked for Clark in the end zone.

Not that this should be surprising—with the physical toll on players in the modern era, football lives are measured in something along the lines of dogs’ years. And while Montana is getting around, he too pays a price today. “I ended up with a new shoulder, a new knee which is just coming around after a year, and a neck fusion,” he says. “I had to rehab injuries when I was playing and more so now. The reason that I retired early was to be able to stay healthy. I wanted to still participate in things while the kids were still growing up. The injuries kind of hurt me on that end of it.”

Montana is honest enough to ensure that the Peacock doc doesn't ignore more painful moments—injuries that if he listened to his orthopedic surgeon would have ended his career at its midway point, the personal wound when the 49ers signed his designated successor Steve Young, landing in Kansas City for his final two seasons, which were actually more productive and rewarding than most people remember. He admits to a few regrets. "I wish I hadn't been as involved in my sons' football careers as I was," he says. "I should have been like my own father—shown them the fundamentals, throwing the ball around with them and leaving the coaching to someone else." That there's no lifelong grudges aired out, no scores settled, is simply a function of his personality. In life as in crisis, he was and remains the calm center with chaos around him. And you suspect that when Joe Montana watches Cool Under Pressure, he’ll be studying it like it was a canister of film that Bill Walsh handed him.
