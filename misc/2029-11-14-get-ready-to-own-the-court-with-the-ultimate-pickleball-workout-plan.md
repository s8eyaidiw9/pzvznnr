# Get Ready to Own the Court With the Ultimate Pickleball Workout Plan

PICKLEBALL HAS EXPLODED in popularity over the past few years. Though the sport was originally invented in 1965, pickleball had a revival during the Covid-19 pandemic when Americans were looking for new ways to socialize outdoors. And while the sport has a reputation for attracting an older crowd, the Association of Pickleball Players (APP) estimated in 2023 that nearly 50 million Americans of all ages played pickleball in the previous year (spoiler: the average age was around 34 years old!), with even more people more finding the sport since then.

Pickleball might be more widely known for its social aspects, but the sport can also provide some profound physical health benefits. A 2022 study found that compared to walking, playing pickleball doubles increased players’ caloric expenditure by 36 percent, their perceived exertion by 44 percent, and their “enjoyment levels” by 150 percent. More research from the 2023 Apple Heart and Movement Study, which used data collected from 82,809 Apple Watch users from November 2019 to November 2021, showed that the benefits of pickleball even mirror those of its higher-intensity cousin, tennis. Players of both sports averaged a peak heart rate within 70 percent of their estimated max heart rate—that is, the maximum number of times your heart can beat per minute during exercise. (A simple method of finding your estimated max heart rate: subtract your age from 220.) Regular exercise like this that gets your heart pumping leads to improved cardiovascular health, increased cognitive function, and a significantly lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease.

To get those physical benefits, most people don’t realize how much athleticism pickleball requires. You may play the game at a lower intensity than other racquet sports like tennis due to the smaller court size and lighter ball, but before you think about mastering your technical skills on the court, you should focus on building rotational power, improving agility, and increasing speed, all while fortifying your body against injuries. Whether you’re training for a marathon, playing basketball with your buddies, or hitting the pickleball courts, you need to incorporate strength training into your routine. Strength training has profound benefits; it can increase your bone density, decrease injury risk, and improve the force production of your muscles.

“There's a reason every other sport is strength training,” says Ty Woody, C.S.C.S., founder of Pickleball Sports Performance and the coach to top players, including International Racquetball Federation (IRF) 2024 World Champion Daniel De La Rosa. “I tell my people, ‘You’re an athlete, you need to train like one.’ The more athletic you are, the more strength you can produce, how well you can absorb force, the better off you’ll be.”

And if you’re worried about how it will affect your game, remember that strength training will help safeguard you against injuries on the court. Pickleball injuries are increasing as the sport continues to grow in popularity. The most common injuries are falls. Data collected from emergency visits show that joint sprains, muscle sprains, and fractures are the most common injuries among pickleballers. 4 Pickleball Workout Principles

Hitting the gym to improve at your sport requires a different approach than training to get stronger and leaner. If you want your work in the gym to translate to the court, you need a plan that mirrors how you move during a match. That’s precisely what we’ve built—a comprehensive, four-week program based on four key training principles.

Rotation

Every time you follow through a forehand, reach across yourself for a backhand, and load up to explode laterally, your body rotates. That’s why each workout in this plan ends with a rotational exercise. You’ll cap off the week with a dedicated rotational training day to help your body twist more efficiently, leading to snappier reactions and more powerful shots.

“Rotation comes from the hips, core, and upper back,” says Woody “If you can't rotate from those parts, you're going to use something else, and that turns into the shoulder or the back.”

Lateral Movement

Pickleball is a game of constant side-to-side movement. Whether shuffling to return a shot or lunging for a dink, your ability to move laterally can make or break your performance. This program incorporates plenty of lateral exercises (think lateral lunges, side planks, and shuffle drills) to bolster your ability to cut and pivot.

Explosivity

You don’t need a 40-inch vertical, but the more force you can generate, the more oomph you can put behind every move—from sprinting to the baseline to rocketing a return. Power starts from the lower body and is driven primarily through the hips, so this program includes kettlebell swings, broad jumps, and Romanian deadlifts to strengthen those force generators.

Unilateral Training

Every shuffle, sprint, and backpedal shifts your weight from one leg to the other, often in a split second. That’s why unilateral exercises (movements performed on one side at a time) are a staple in this program. They’ll help correct muscle imbalances, improve stability, and build strength where you need it most. You’ll utilize unilateral exercises like the kettlebell windmill and alternating bench press.

Program Overview

This routine has you training three days per week, each workout lasting about 40 minutes. Pick the days that fit your schedule, but rest at least one day between workouts. On three other days, you have options: go for a 20-minute run, play pickleball, or, if your body’s asking for it, rest. One full rest day is mandatory each week to ensure you’re recovered and ready to tackle the next round of training.

Samuel designed three distinct training days: total-body strength, athleticism, and rotational movement. Unlike a traditional weightlifting plan, this program swaps out barbell movements for explosive, plyometric, and agility-focused exercises that build stamina, coordination, and rotational power, all of the traits crucial for pivoting into a serve or twisting into a backhand.

You won’t find many free-weight exercises here—except for moves like chest-supported rows and offset-stance Romanian deadlifts—because the focus is on moving like an athlete.

What You Need for the Ultimate Pickleball Workout Program

Because this program emphasizes explosive bodyweight movements, you don’t need much equipment to follow it. You’ll need a pair of dumbbells, a kettlebell, a resistance band with handles, a looped resistance band (to wear around your knees), and preferably an adjustable weight bench—so you might be best served taking on these workouts at a gym.
