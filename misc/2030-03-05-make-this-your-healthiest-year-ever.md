# Make This Your Healthiest Year Ever

If hangovers lasted 6 months, quitting would be a lot easier. But by our clock, you'll probably be ready to mix another martini by 1 p.m., January 3. Here's a trick: Next time you get drunk, write down everything you drink. The average guy knocks back about three more alcoholic drinks per month than a decade ago, and even more during the holidays, says Tom Greenfield, Ph.D., director of the Alcohol Research Group's national center, in Emeryville, California. Researchers there found that those who kept a record of their consumption tended to drink less. "Once they keep a tally, people are often surprised to find out how much they drink," he says.

Exercise should be your first option--it's a proven weapon. Depression can be the result of low levels of activity in your frontal (thinking) lobes and high levels in your limbic (emotional) system. "Brain scans show that psychotherapy can restore the balance," says Norman Doidge, M.D., author of The Brain That Changes Itself. Or you can try to retrain your brain.

Be a Depression Detective. Transient bouts of the blues can sometimes be lifted, if you find out what's triggering them. "Determine exactly when your funk began," says Dr. Doidge. If the answer isn't obvious, use free association to suss out the source. Did something remind you of a past disappointment? Write a letter to yourself or clear your head and see what comes to mind.

Stop Obsessing. Everyone has a mechanism to notice mistakes, an anxiety center to prompt responses, and a gearshift in the brain to move on. "In worry-prone people, that gearshift gets stuck," says Dr. Doidge. Manually shift with a distracting wholesome activity: Read a book, solve a Sudoku, or organize the garage. "This grows neuronal connections in that part of the brain, so the gearshift becomes more automatic."

Admit You're Angry. Holding it in hurts. Chronic suppressed anger can turn off one of the brain's major antianxiety switches," Dr. Doidge says. Determine whether your anger is justified, then deal with the cause.

A three-pronged proactive defense

Work

You spend huge amounts of time at work, so the first step is to evaluate the setup of your desk, chair, and computer, says Darran Marlow, D.C., director of the chiropractic division of the Texas Back Institute. Find a chair you can sit in with both feet flat on the floor, and set your computer monitor at eye level, Dr. Marlow says, and "75 percent of your neck and back problems will improve." Prop one foot up on a footrest or phone book, and switch feet when you get uncomfortable--this will decrease the pressure on your lower back and help prevent discomfort. And rise out of your chair often--that can be the key to preventing workplace-related pain.

Work Out

A combination of cardio work and back-strengthening exercise will also help prevent pain, says Rick Deyo, M.D. "Your legs, hips, and back should all be in good shape," he says. Try weight training, yoga, and/or Pilates--their core-strengthening moves will help support your spine. However, beware of exercise backfiring: Lifting weights with poor form can really cost you, Dr. Marlow says. "Take the time to learn how to appropriately execute an exercise." To start, check out our video demonstrations at MensHealth.com, keyword back. Then schedule a session with a trainer.

Sleep

At night, sleep on your side with a pillow between your knees or, if you can stand it, lie on your back with a pillow under your knees, says Dr. Marlow--you'll significantly decrease the pressure on your lower back.

You can accomplish both with your eyes closed. The answer: meditation. No, it's not just for wifty mystics. Cripes, Howard Stern is into meditation. Lots of studies prove that it works. The latest was published in the Archives of Internal Medicine; participants' systolic blood pressure dropped 3 points after they completed a meditation program. "Meditating neutralizes stress, improving the body's ability to regulate blood pressure," says Stuart Rothenberg, M.D., director of the Transcendental Meditation program of South Florida. ("TM" was the type of meditation used in the study--and by the Beatles, but don't hold that against it.) Another recent study found that exercise and relaxation together lower blood pressure better than exercise alone. TM takes just two 20-minute sessions a day. You find a distraction-free spot, sit, close your eyes, and repeat a single-word mantra to block out thought.

You know a packful of methods are out there, but "there are no magic bullets," says Michael Fiore, M.D., director of the center for tobacco research and intervention at the University of Wisconsin medical school. Do it now, not tomorrow, because the longer you put it off, Sparky, the harder it'll be. Here are five real-world bullets that can help you end the madness.

1. Don't set yourself up for failure. It may be great for sandwiches the day after Thanksgiving, but cold turkey probably won't get you anywhere when it comes to kicking your butts, says Dr. Fiore. Neither will hypnosis nor acupuncture, including the latter's latest iteration, aiming lasers at acupuncture points. "Those are probably the three worst ways to quit."

2. Go pharmacological. Prescription quitting aids have improved. The FDA has approved Pfizer's Chantix, a new quit-smoking pill with a one-two punch: Chantix mimics nicotine's effects while simultaneously blocking the reinforcing effects of the nicotine you take in from cigarettes.

3. Lay off the bottle, too. Don't clear out just cigs, lighters, and ashtrays. Toss as much booze as you're willing to part with, too: "Half of people who quit and turn back take their first drag with alcohol in their bloodstream," Dr. Fiore says. Eliminate alcohol for the first couple of months after quitting. The other predictor of a relapse is living with a smoker, so if you can convince your wife or roomie to quit while you're at it, you'll both have a better chance.

4. Delay the game. When you get a craving you can't seem to shake, promise yourself you won't give in for 60 minutes. Almost every time, the urge will pass by the time the hour's up. If you have to, spend the hour drinking water, walking around, doing deep- breathing exercises, or chewing on something, Dr. Fiore says. "Aim for something you can chew on without taking in a lot of extra calories," since it's common to gain a few pounds when you quit. Try carrots. (See "Reduce Your Cancer Risk.")

5. Sweat. Whatever method you try, combine it with exercise. A new study by the American College of Chest Physicians shows that smokers who combine exercise with nicotine gum or patches are more likely to quit than those who don't exercise.

There is no good reason for you not to have a physical. And there are two big reasons you should. One, chances are, there's nothing wrong with you, so stop being a chicken. Then why have a physical? Baselines, that's why. The doctor will establish baseline readings of your weight, blood pressure, cholesterol, electrocardiogram, and (if family history or age makes it a concern) PSA score. Make this one visit and you can let it ride, says Harvey Simon, M.D., an internist at Massachusetts General Hospital and the author of The Harvard Medical School Guide to Men's Health. You need to check back only every 5 years in your 20s, every 3 in your 30s, and every 2 in your 40s. And with one physical, you've also established a baseline relationship with your doctor, which you'll want when something really does go wrong.

Write down your aches, pains, and history so you don't blank at the doc's.

Worry about the right thing first: The closer you can bring your HDL (good) number to your LDL (bad), the better. The best first steps are exercise (the vigorous kind boosts HDL) and diet, before you fall back on statins. An easy fix: A Turkish study discovered that eating an ounce of hazelnuts a day decreased LDL by 30 percent and raised HDL by 12 percent. But almost any nut can work--eat a few throughout the day for the best effect.

Do not attach yourself to an IV drip of Airborne. Instead, figure out where the germs most likely to attack you are lurking. Then protect yourself with exercise (the easiest, smartest immune booster there is), acknowledge the germ carriers, and fight back.

Charles Hirshberg : My Resolution For 2007

"I will thrive without TV"

I killed my roommate. Believe me, it was a justifiable homicide. This lowlife slug never helped with the rent, but constantly nagged me to buy all kinds of expensive junk I didn't need. He was an obnoxious little peckerhead who always seemed to be shouting irritating, unfunny jokes about nookie and poo, and then squealing with laughter, hoping I'd join in.

You've probably guessed: The peckerheaded roommate was my TV set, and I got a head start on the New Year by throwing that sucker out on its wide-screen ass.

If I can muster the self-discipline not to let this time-sucking appliance back into my home, 2007 may be my best year yet.

TV is infinitely more insidious and corrosive than any human being: It hypnotizes you into stupidity, then softly unhooks your will, replacing it with its own. What is that, if not a horror movie come to life? Scientists say 2 hours a day can make you fat and diabetic, to say nothing of boring, dumb, and crazy. One harrowing example: A professor of psychology lured a gaggle of famished undergraduates into his laboratory by offering pizza or macaroni and cheese. The students who ate while watching TV inhaled 36 percent more pizza than they did while listening to music, and an astounding 71 percent more mac and cheese.

For me, there was no way to cut back, except to exterminate the thing. You'd better believe I missed it at first, especially sports, cartoons, and the parade of hotties. But now I think everybody should try eliminating it, if only for a week or two.

Make a list of all the stuff you'd like to do with the time you're spending in front of the tube. Not just healthy stuff, but goofy, downtime stuff, too. For me, that meant playing the guitar at the Irish bar down the street, spending time with my buddies, and, about once a week, wandering down to the park, sitting on a bench, and watching the real-life parade of hotties.
