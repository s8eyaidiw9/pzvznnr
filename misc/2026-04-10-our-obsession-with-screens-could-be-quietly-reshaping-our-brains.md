Spatial Awareness Processing Disorder is an effect of modern living, not an official medical diagnosis.

CHANCES ARE, YOU spend a good chunk of your day staring at a screen. Sometimes you'll get blurry, tired eyes. Sometimes your butt gets sore from sitting. That should be the extent of the dangers, right?

But if you've been clumsier lately, feeling disoriented in unfamiliar places, or relying on GPS even on familiar routes—you may also have a case of Spatial Awareness Processing Disorder (SAPD).

Never heard of SAPD? You're not alone. It's a new condition coined by Meenal Agarwal, OD, optometrist and host of the Uncover Your Eyes podcast. Essentially, she says excess screentime could be reshaping your brain, causing a loss of spatial awareness. This is the cognitive ability to understand your relationship to the objects around you. Spatial awareness is the reason you can move through a room without bumping into anything, or pour coffee into a cup without spilling.

But SAPD is not a 'disease,' Agarwal says. Instead, it's a consequence of living a screen-focused lifestyle with limited movement. And it's fixable. Here’s what you should know.

What is Spatial Awareness Processing Disorder?

SAPD DESCRIBES CHANGES in how the brain uses visual information to process spatial, peripheral, and navigational information, Agarwal wrote in a blog post published in November.

The idea stems from “patterns” she’s noticed in her patients over the past few years, including shrinking peripheral vision and depth perception. For instance, people bumping into objects more often, kids struggling to play sports, and teens being unable to judge distances well while driving. Adults are showing weaker navigation skills, she says, even in areas they've been through plenty of times before.

Many of these behaviors could be from anxiety, Agarwal acknowledges. However, she thinks more of them are likely attributed to two things. First, reduced movement in real-life settings. Second, relying on screens that lock your eyes into a near space instead of your surroundings. By focusing on a 2D flat object (that is, your screens) all day, “we’re not able to process our space around us,” she explains.

The Theory Behind Spatial Awareness Processing Disorder

When you look straight ahead, like when staring at a phone, you're using a fraction of your visual field, Agarwal explains. The information you see goes to a specific area of the retina (known as the fovea centralis), which feeds into the brain for processing.

The fovea centralis is composed of cone cells that process details such as color and lines in the brain. Everything around the fovea, the peripheral retina, is composed of rod cells, which process motion, depth perception, and movement.

“When you’re constantly exercising the foveal pathway, you’ve diluted your peripheral pathway,” Agarwal says. “Just like the rest of our body, if you’re not using it, it gets weak.”

Keep in mind, SAPD hasn’t been officially studied in research labs yet. Right now, it's considered a “hypothesis with a clinical pattern,” as Agarwal puts it. She’s planning to kick off a study on the condition next year.

Screen use and its impact on spatial awareness and brain/eye health have been under investigation for some time now. Agarwal says the idea for SAPD aligns with existing research on spatial cognition and GPS dependence, screen time and minimized motor development in children, spatial awareness and driving confidence, and higher screen time in children related to lower visual-spatial scores.

What Other Doctors Think About SAPD

SAPD IS “INTERESTING but unproven,” says Andrew Lee, MD, a neuro-ophthalmologist at Houston Methodist.

[Screens Could Be Quietly](https://incretin-path.escortskhi.com/blog/0501187942.html)

“There is a biologically plausible mechanism for the reduction in spatial awareness from excessive viewing of flat screen technology, but correlation is not the same as causation,” Lee explains. He suggests another reason such as decreased outdoor activity rather than increased virtual activity as an alternative cause.

Lee says a two-dimensional projection of a 3D object could theoretically alter spatial awareness even for a fully formed adult brain. “Like the use-it-or-lose-it mantra, lack of use of 3D spatial awareness could lead to degradation of ability over time.”

Spatial awareness problems would be one of the many issues associated with excess screentime. Lee explains screen overuse can cause eye strain, photosensitivity, headaches, and sleep disturbances. Studies also suggest that too much screen time can increase the risk of nearsightedness and negatively affect socialization, communication, and real human interactions.

How to Counteract The Effects of Screen Overuse

START BY TAKING screen breaks, Lee says. This is likely easier said than done, as most people report spending at least five hours a day looking at screens. Here are some tips to help:

Follow the 20/20/20 rule

Every 20 minutes, take a 20-second break to look at something 20 feet away, suggests James Kelly, MD, ophthalmologist and eye surgeon in New York City. “This helps relax the eye muscles and reduce digital eye strain. Don’t forget to blink frequently during screen use.”

Spend time outdoors

Spending more time in 3D environments—walking, playing sports, or just looking around outside—will exercise your peripheral vision, Agarwal says. “That’s strengthening your spatial awareness.”

Position your screens properly

If you work from home, Agarwal suggests positioning your workstation near a window so you can look outside periodically. Also, Kelly recommends using anti-glare filters and night mode to reduce visual stress and positioning screens at eye level.
