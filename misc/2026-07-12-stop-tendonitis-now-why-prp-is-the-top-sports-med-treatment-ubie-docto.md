Healing Tendonitis: Why PRP Is the Preferred Sports Med Treatment

Tendonitis—an inflammation or irritation of a tendon—can derail even the most dedicated athlete or active individual. Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) has emerged as a leading treatment in sports medicine for speeding recovery, reducing pain, and promoting long-term tendon health. This article explains why PRP for tendonitis is often preferred over traditional options, outlines what to expect, and helps you decide if it's right for you.

Understanding Tendonitis

Tendons connect muscle to bone. When overuse or direct injury causes small tears or chronic inflammation, you may experience:

Localized pain and tenderness

Swelling or stiffness

Reduced range of motion

A sensation of "grinding" or crackling

Common sites include the Achilles tendon, rotator cuff (shoulder), patellar tendon (knee), tennis elbow (lateral epicondyle), and golfer's elbow (medial epicondyle).

Traditional Treatments and Their Limitations

Standard first-line approaches often involve:

Rest and activity modification

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

Physical therapy and targeted exercises

Corticosteroid injections

While these strategies can offer short-term relief, they frequently fall short of promoting true tissue regeneration. In some cases, repeated steroid injections may weaken tendon structure over time, raising the risk of rupture.

What Is PRP?

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) is a concentration of your own platelets suspended in a small volume of plasma. Platelets contain growth factors and cytokines that help regulate inflammation and kick-start the body's natural healing cascade.

Key points about PRP:

Autologous: Prepared from your own blood, minimizing risk of allergic reaction

Concentrated Growth Factors: Delivers a higher dose of healing agents than blood normally carries

Minimally Invasive: Injected directly into or around the injured tendon

How PRP Works for Tendonitis

Blood Draw & Centrifugation– A small blood sample is taken from your arm and spun in a centrifuge.– This separates red blood cells, white blood cells, and plasma.– The platelet-rich layer is extracted for injection.

Injection into the Injury Site– Using ultrasound guidance, the physician injects PRP precisely at the site of tendon damage.– Growth factors (e.g., PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF) are released over days to weeks.

Healing Cascade Activation– Platelets recruit stem cells and promote collagen synthesis.– Inflammation is modulated to avoid chronic irritation.– Damaged tendon fibers are remodeled with healthier tissue.

Evidence Supporting PRP for Tendonitis

Clinical studies show promising outcomes:

Achilles Tendinopathy: A randomized trial found PRP led to greater pain reduction and functional improvement at six months compared with saline injections.

Lateral Epicondylitis (Tennis Elbow): Several controlled studies report significant pain relief and lower recurrence at one year post-PRP.

Patellar Tendinopathy: Athletes receiving PRP returned to sport faster than those on conservative therapy alone.

Meta-analyses conclude that PRP for tendonitis offers a favorable risk-benefit profile, especially when traditional measures fail.

Benefits of PRP for Tendonitis

Accelerated Healing: Direct delivery of growth factors stimulates tissue repair.

Reduced Pain: Many patients report sustained pain relief beyond what NSAIDs or steroids achieve.

Minimal Downtime: Most athletes resume light activity within a week.

Low Allergy Risk: Since PRP is autologous, immune reactions are rare.

Long-Term Results: By promoting actual tendon regeneration, PRP may lower risk of future flare-ups.

What to Expect During PRP Treatment

Initial Consultation– Review medical history, imaging studies (ultrasound/MRI), and discuss goals.

Blood Draw & Processing– Outpatient procedure lasting about 30 minutes.

Injection Session– Ultrasound-guided PRP injection into the tendon.– Mild discomfort or pressure is normal.

Post-Injection Care– Brief rest (24–48 hours), then a structured rehab plan.– Physical therapy typically begins one week after injection.

Risks and Considerations

PRP is generally safe, but you should be aware of:

Infection: Extremely rare with proper sterile technique.

Pain at Injection Site: Temporary increase in discomfort for 48–72 hours.

No Instant Fix: Full benefits develop over 6–12 weeks.

Who Is a Good Candidate?

You may consider PRP for tendonitis if you:

Have persistent tendon pain > 3–6 months

Have tried rest, NSAIDs, bracing, or steroids without lasting relief

Are willing to follow a structured rehab and activity-modification program

Are in generally good health (no active infections or blood disorders)

Some conditions, such as advanced tendon degeneration or tears exceeding 50% of tendon cross-section, may require surgical repair instead.

Aftercare and Recovery

Phase 1 (Weeks 0–2): Rest, avoid heavy lifting or high-impact activity.

Phase 2 (Weeks 2–6): Begin gentle range-of-motion and isometric exercises.

Phase 3 (Weeks 6–12): Progress to strengthening, eccentric loading, and sport-specific drills.

Phase 4 (3+ Months): Return to full activity, guided by symptom improvement and clinician approval.

Compliance with rehabilitation protocols is crucial to maximize PRP benefits.

Next Steps: Monitoring and Follow-Up

Regular follow-up appointments allow your sports medicine physician to:

Assess pain levels and functional gains

Adjust rehab exercises

Repeat imaging if progress stalls

Consider a second PRP session in select cases
