# Sick in the Head

The package arrives in a plain brown wrapper. I sneak it up to the bedroom and close the door. My wife, Debbie, who has endured my obsession for decades, must not suspect what I'm up to.

As I unbundle the Merck Manual of Medical Information, Second Home Edition, I simultaneously inventory my current symptomatic load. This includes dyspnea (breathlessness often associated with lung disorders), palpitations (possibly indicative of Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome), and globus hystericus (inexplicable lump in the throat).

Anxiety alone, of course, could explain all these things.

More likely, the real cause will be found within this 1,907-page, best-selling compendium of medical disasters — a layman's guide to everything that can and so often does go wrong with the human body, from Mönckeberg's arteriosclerosis to bird fancier's lung.

Ever since my early adulthood, my friends and family have acknowledged that I suffer from something. It's just not, they maintain, the assortment of ripped-from-the-headlines infirmities I think I have, from SARS and anthrax to avian flu and E. coli O157:H7.

The more charitable of my armchair diagnosticians suggest I'm simply part of the contemporary "worried well" phenomenon that's tainted legions of fellow Americans with pervasive health anxieties. Others, however, argue that my case transcends "worried well" to "worried ill." Their label for me: clinical hypochondria.

To this latter charge, I've always rejoined, "I'm no hypo — just sickly."

Alas, lately I've begun to wonder. A recent biopsy of a penile lesion, almost certainly triggered by herpes and/or flesh-eating strep, turned out to be a mosquito bite. I wish I could say I found this reassuring, but within a half hour of hearing the result, I'd begun to imagine the devastation of West Nile virus on the male urogenital tract.

Hence the current experiment. If I'm truly a hypo, surely reading the Merck Manual cover to cover will flush me out. Before beginning Chapter 1, however, I open the book at random, hoping to reassure myself that I'm not vulnerable to every symptom I read. As luck would have it, my finger lands in the section on groin disorders, a particular bugaboo of mine.

When the text describes a "varicocele" as feeling "like a bag of worms" in a guy's scrotum, I suddenly can't escape the sensation that nightcrawlers have begun breeding in my ball sack.

There's always been a subset of guys who take well-being so seriously, it hurts their health. Charles Darwin, Immanuel Kant, Samuel Johnson, and Abraham Lincoln are just a few of the historical figures/episodic invalids thought to have suffered from hypochondria. This condition, originally described by Hippocrates, derives its name from the Greek for "under the ribs" — a locus for the multiple gastrointestinal complaints so often a hallmark of the disorder.

What concerns a growing cadre of contemporary researchers and clinicians alike is that morbid health preoccupations — be they born of hard-core hypochondria per se or the less delusional worried-well variation — no longer appear limited to a small, pathological subfraction. Rather, cases seem to be exploding in modern America, arguably the healthiest time and place humans have ever lived.

In an article in the New England Journal of Medicine entitled "The Paradox of Health," Arthur Barsky, M.D., a professor of psychiatry at Harvard medical school, documents how the nation's objective health and subjective perceptions of well-being have been moving in opposite directions for years. "Despite substantial improvements in our health status," Dr. Barsky found, "people now report higher rates of disability, symptoms, and general dissatisfaction with their health."

Among other lines of evidence, Dr. Barsky cites statistics showing that in 1928, doctors could effectively treat or prevent only 5 percent to 10 percent of the 360 most serious human diseases. Despite this, in large-scale community surveys at the time, the average respondent reported having suffered just one episode of serious, acute, or disabling illness per year. Fifty years later, medicine had advanced to the point where doctors could successfully treat or prevent 50 percent to 55 percent of the same serious diseases. And yet Americans at this point reported averaging two illnesses per year — double the burden of their ancestors. If anything, say experts, the problem continues to worsen.

"It's not that our forebears didn't care about being healthy," says Arthur Caplan, Ph.D., director of the center for bioethics at the University of Pennsylvania. "It's that they tended to view good health as a function of God's will, not something they could control as human beings." Why worry if there's nothing you can do?

But, as medical advances have increasingly provided the sense that we can control our health destiny, more and more people are embracing the message that we must take every step to do so, including monitoring each fleeting twinge or vague discomfort for signs of early trouble.

And nothing has made it easier — or more tempting — to self- diagnose than the Internet and the instant access to medical information it provides. The Merck Manual itself is not only available online but easy to search as well. The next time you have a headache, for instance, you can discover within nanoseconds how your "cephalalgia" might indicate "acute systemic or intracranial infection, intracranial tumor, head injuries, severe hypertension, cerebral hypoxia, and many diseases of the eyes, nose, throat, teeth, ears, and cervical vertebrae."

"The Internet is a major problem for many of our patients," says Dr. Barsky. "They go online with the hope that they're going to reassure themselves, but most of the time, they end up more worried than when they started."

A few weeks into my medical self-education, virtually everything I find in the Merck Manual continues to scare the bejeezus out of me. Before beginning today's chapter on brain disorders, I execute what has become a mandatory prereading ritual. I pop a baby aspirin to reduce the odds of infarction, lie down on the couch in the event that excessive vagus-nerve stimulation triggers orthostatic hypotension, take a few deep, cleansing breaths, and begin.

Immediately, my fear-dilated pupils alight on a map of the human brain depicting how damage to different regions impairs us. Injury to the right parietal lobe, for example, can cause apraxia, or an inability to perform simple skilled tasks, such as getting dressed or brushing your hair. I can't help but notice that it's now 11 a.m. and not only am I still in my pajamas, but random spikes of what little hair I have remaining jut out at crazy angles from my head. I swallow hard and move on.

It turns out I'm hardly the only guy swept up in the net of modern health anxiety. The ironic perception of increased vulnerability to myriad reapers — in an age when so much can be done to keep them at bay — has even begun afflicting a demographic niche long thought too stoical to even notice their health: young men in the prime of life.

"It's unquestionable that many guys today are worrying much more about health than previous generations of men did," says Marc Siegel, M.D., an associate professor at New York University school of medicine and the author of False Alarm: The Truth about the Epidemic of Fear.

From media-hyped "bugs du jour" like bird flu and mad cow disease to what Dr. Siegel calls "the personal terrorists" of cancer and heart disease, the zeitgeist has transformed over the past several decades. Contrast yesteryear's gut-shot war hero, casually taking a drag on his cigarette before dismissing a mortal wound as "only a scratch," with today's health-fanatical triathlete, driven to a hissy fit by the effect an errant sniffle might have on his training regimen.

"Males of the last generation were very prone to suppress and deny their symptoms," says Dr. Siegel. "It just wasn't macho to worry about these things. Today, it's become okay to be frightened about your health. Fear has supplanted courage — maybe pigheadedness is a better term — as the emotion in vogue."

On the plus side, the proliferation of health information has cut down on the worst of male denial, leading more men than ever to seek help when they really need it. But too much info bombardment, especially when delivered without any context about realistic risk and/or tailored to suit the hidden agendas of fear profiteers (more on this in a moment), is spawning a growing chorus of otherwise robust men who go through life whistling at graveyards.

Whatever the worrier's gender, the dividing line between "normal" and "pathological" is not always obvious. Perhaps the main difference lies in a patient's reassurability. For clinical hypochondriacs, no amount of testing or medical reassurance reliably shakes them of their doomsday convictions. They tend to believe that the lab botched the test, or the doctor is too incompetent to find the underlying disease.

The worried well, on the other hand, can respond rationally to test results and sound doctorly counsel — provided, that is, they see a practitioner who can spend the time to truly educate them. Alas, this is hardly the norm in today's HMO-dominated medical assembly line.

Thanks to managed care, says Dr. Siegel, many physicians start feeling antsy if a consultation lasts longer than 15 minutes. "Doctors today just don't have the time to become engaged enough with their patients."

Between true hypochondriacs who can't be persuaded that their fears are unfounded, and worried-well patients unable to find effectively reassuring doctors, the quest for relief from imagined illness has become a huge business. Dr. Barsky's research suggests that up to 20 percent of the nation's total health-care expenditure — some $256 billion annually — goes into treating symptoms with no serious medical basis.

In one span of a few hours, I study everything from brain herniation to the near-biblical damnation of the "locked-in state." In the latter condition, victims of extreme paralysis find it nearly impossible to communicate with the outside world. The very thought of being thus caged within my own dank cognitive sphere — unable to recall enough Morse code to eye-blink "Smother me!" to Nurse Ratched — triggers my heart into a jackrabbity rhythm.

This, of course, only recalls another recently discovered and impossible-to-forget medical horror show — the myxoma. The Merck Manual describes this as a jellylike tumor dangling from a stalk inside the heart's pumping chamber. "With standing," the authors explain, "the force of gravity pulls the myxoma into the opening of the mitral valve, blocking blood flow through the heart." With each desperate palpitation, I feel the lethal tetherball swinging amok in my atrium.

In 1985, the FDA lifted a long-standing ban on direct-to-consumer (DTC) advertising of prescription drugs, at which point the United States joined New Zealand as one of only two nations in the industrialized world to allow Rx marketing. Still, it took a while for things to get rolling in America: By 1991, drug companies were collectively spending a modest $55 million on TV, print, and radio campaigns. This climbed to $2.5 billion by 2000, with Men's Health being one of the countless media outlets accepting the ads. Why the boom? In part because appeals to the worried well were proving so effective. From 1996 to 2000, the number of prescriptions dispensed to the average American climbed by more than two per year, to 11.

Consumer surveys now show that during a third of all office visits, patients plead for prescriptions for drugs they've seen advertised on TV. Up to 69 percent of the time, they get the brand they ask for.

"Every day," says Dr. Siegel, "I treat patients with ad-driven cravings for a drug. These ads are very good at creating what I call 'fear dependencies' — the sense that if you don't get your hands on this magic elixir, you're going to get or stay sick."

Evangelists for drug advertising, on the other hand, claim that direct marketing serves patients who otherwise might never learn about the latest breakthrough treatments. For disorders like depression, which most mental-health experts believe is woefully underdiagnosed and even less likely to be effectively treated, such an argument may seem valid, especially if ads inspire real hope in those who are hurting.

But an analysis published in 2004 by researchers at the University of Washington department of medicine questioned this assumption. They concluded that the information contained in most antidepressant ads is of poor quality and targeted to patients whose very conditions leave them most vulnerable to a sales pitch. Perhaps not surprisingly, 18 percent of the 50 most heavily advertised drugs in the United States are for just such psychiatric and neurological problems.

Primary-care doctors, to be sure, are the ostensible guardians of the prescription pad, and many do try to rein in their patients' drug demands. Still, the reality is that the worried-well person stymied by one gatekeeper can almost always find another doctor willing to give him what he wants. Faced with the threat of losing patients, and barraged by the salesmanship of pharmaceutical reps, many doctors today find it easier just to take the path of least resistance.

Of course, it's not just serious disorders like depression and heart disease that rampant "medicohype" has conditioned us to fear. Consider the bombardment of ads for lifestyle-enhancement drugs. We males are suckers for the "be all that you can be" appeals of everything from antibaldness meds to testosterone gels. These may not create full-blown intimations of mortality the way cancer and cholesterol ads do. (Who can forget the "It's your future — be there!" campaign for Zocor?) But so-called lifestyle drug ads inflame another kind of health fear to which men are particularly susceptible: the idea that we won't measure up.

Even if you've never been impotent, be honest: If you could spend a night with that seductive Cialis babe, wouldn't you want those cost-be-damned tablets as insurance for a "longer, stronger" experience, if only because you know she's expecting a drug-boosted peak performance?

"Pharmaceutical companies like to sell drugs that are used frequently," says bioethicist Caplan. "That's why you see a lot of ads for toenail-fungus remedies and erectile-dysfunction pills. The subliminal message is 'Maybe you're okay — but things could be better.'"

"Diseases of Unknown Cause" covers a quartet of well-publicized syndromes — chronic fatigue, multiple chemical sensitivity, sick-building, and Gulf War — each of which I've feared suffering, most intensely during their respective heydays in the popular media.

Symptoms of Gulf War syndrome, for example, include problems with memory, reasoning, concentration, and attention; difficulty falling asleep; depression; fatigue; and headache. Plus, on occasion, disorientation, dizziness, erectile dysfunction, muscle pains, muscle fatigue, weakness, diarrhea, skin rashes, cough, and chest pain.

Except for the fact that I've never been within 3,000 miles of the Persian Gulf, it's uncanny how precisely this laundry list describes what it's like to be me. Might I have somehow become infected by watching too much war coverage on CNN? There's no denying that each new dispatch from Iraq makes me sick.

Whether real or imagined, the sickness of one man is the fortune of another. Caplan and other experts point out that DTC advertising is only one high-profile aspect of a massive, interconnected system that seeks to "educate" us about the multitude of health problems crying out for remedy. According to the National Coalition on Health Care, the United States spent 15.3 percent of its gross domestic product on health care in 2003, and this percentage is expected to reach 18.7 percent in 10 years. Health care has become a voracious cash cow that feeds on patient demand, and to ensure that this demand will grow, it's in the interest of numerous players to keep our fears stoked.

"A giant medical-industrial complex has arisen," Dr. Barsky sums up in his NEJM article. This colossus comprises everything from for-profit health-care corporations and freestanding diagnostic labs to nutraceutical empires and boutique health clinics shilling their cures. "Each producer," he suggests, "tries to convince the public that something is dangerously wrong, or about to go wrong, and that immediate steps must be taken to remedy the situation."

Drug makers, hospital conglomerates, and the like may profit directly from selling us health care, but plenty of remoras have found indirect niches from which to extract profits as well. "From the local news to national health coverage, the media makes a good chunk of its living touting unlikely health problems to worry about," says Caplan.

A strange disease that kills only a few people still makes for good headlines if the story is strategically hyped. Many evening-news teasers employ the line "Are you and your family at risk?" The answer, of course, is usually no, but the tagline generates enough heebie-jeebies to keep viewers from switching over to Seinfeld reruns.

Another technique used by print and TV journalists alike is to dramatize the latest scary affliction by depicting an individual sufferer's travails. Such personalization definitely hooks people, but, thanks to a quirk of the human brain, it also sets the stage for pervasive unease in the public at large.

Elizabeth Phelps, Ph.D., a fear researcher at New York University, recently used MRI scans to look at a brain structure, the amygdala, which becomes activated in the face of danger. Phelps showed that we don't even need to experience threats firsthand for our amygdalas to be tripped onto high alert. Simply watching someone else's health tragedy unfold on TV can infect us with our own Oh, my god! I'm next! terrors. Such panic, in turn, leads to everything from a run on the antibiotic Cipro during the anthrax scare to widespread use of face masks during the SARS "epidemic."

Rationality and perspective are not always easy to muster, but these may just be the worried well's best hope of escaping an anxious life. Caplan, who, as a bioethicist, is paid to look at the big picture, advises that we all take a step back and stop being such pigeons for fear-mongers. "Health is what we call in ethics an 'instrumental value' — it's desirable because it lets you enjoy other things," he says. "If all you're doing is pursuing health for its own sake, you're missing the point."

It's 4 months since the Merck Manual arrived, and as much as I try to work myself up over the possibility of Gulf War-by-proxy syndrome, I find I just can't do it. True, I have suffered all the enumerated symptoms at various points in my life. But, then again, who hasn't?

A few short months ago, this insouciance would have struck me as impossible. And yet, to my amazement, the sheer accretion of things that can go wrong in the body has slowly lost its grip on me. Tricuspid regurgitation, rabies in both its "furious" and "dumb" forms, pork-tapeworm larvae wriggling about in my brain tissue: all dreadful possibilities, to be sure. But, for whatever reason, the more I've read about such dangers, the more I've viewed them as increasingly unlikely fates.

One of the wisest doctors I've ever met once consoled me thusly: "Jim," he said, "if you're born to hang, you'll never drown." I have no idea what angel of death will one day snatch me, but I just can't bring myself to believe it will take the form of pork-tapeworm larvae.

As my studies of the Merck Manual progressed, something even stranger happened: I started noticing positive factoids about the human body and its near-miraculous capacity for resiliency. Who knew, for instance, that a large portion of the human liver must be destroyed before it hurts our health? Or that agoraphobia — a severe form of anxiety that renders one in 50 men housebound each year — can disappear on its own, without formal treatment, thanks to the ability of daily life itself to act as a potent form of desensitization therapy?

Just maybe, rummaging through this gauntlet of medical nightmares has accomplished the same for my erstwhile hypo soul. I have exposed myself to the full catastrophe, the whole slaughterous cavalcade of documented human illness. Before beginning this project, I was certain that by its conclusion, I'd need months in an asylum to quiet my nerves. But when I finally flip over page 1,907 and close the book for good, I find all I really need is a nap.
