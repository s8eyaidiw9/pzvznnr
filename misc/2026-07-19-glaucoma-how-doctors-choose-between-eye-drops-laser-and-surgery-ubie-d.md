Glaucoma: How Doctors Choose Between Eye Drops, Laser, and Surgery

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Glaucoma treatment is personalized based on glaucoma type, disease severity, target intraocular pressure (IOP), and individual patient factors. The three primary treatment options are:

Prescription eye drops – often the first-line therapy to lower IOP

Laser therapy – such as selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT)

Surgical procedures – including MIGS, trabeculectomy, and tube shunts

Each option carries distinct benefits, risks, and eligibility criteria. Key considerations include medication side effects, lifestyle adjustments, procedural risks, and long-term IOP management. Reviewing these factors with your eye care provider helps determine the safest, most effective path forward.

Because glaucoma symptoms often go unnoticed until vision loss occurs, understanding your risk early is critical—and irreversible damage can be prevented with timely action. Explanation

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases that can lead to vision loss by damaging the optic nerve, often due to high pressure inside the eye. Identifying the right glaucoma treatment options is a personalized decision. Doctors weigh factors like disease type, severity, patient lifestyle and risk factors. This guide explains how specialists decide between eye drops, laser therapy and surgery to help you understand the process and feel confident discussing choices with your doctor.

Understanding Glaucoma and Why Treatment Matters

• Glaucoma usually develops slowly (primary open-angle) but can be sudden (acute angle-closure).• High intraocular pressure (IOP) is the most common treatable risk factor.• Left untreated, glaucoma can cause irreversible vision loss and, in rare cases, blindness.

Early diagnosis and proper management are key. Treatments aim to lower IOP, protect the optic nerve and preserve your remaining vision.

Key Factors Doctors Consider

When choosing among the main glaucoma treatment options, doctors evaluate:

Type of glaucoma• Open-angle vs. angle-closure vs. other forms

Severity and rate of progression

Baseline IOP and target IOP (the pressure needed to slow damage)

Patient factors• Age, overall health, ability to follow treatment• Other eye conditions (cataract, ocular surface disease)• Medication allergies or sensitivities

Lifestyle and adherence• Work schedule, hand-eye coordination, support system

Risk vs. benefit balance• Potential side effects, surgical risks, need for repeat procedures

No single approach fits everyone. Your doctor will discuss the best path based on your exam, test results and personal needs.

Eye Drops: The First-Line Glaucoma Treatment Option

Most patients start with medicated eye drops. They are non-invasive, widely available and can effectively lower IOP.

Laser Therapy: A Minimally Invasive Choice

Laser procedures can be an alternative or addition to drops. They enhance fluid drainage through the eye's natural channels.

Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty (SLT)

Uses low-level laser to target drainage meshwork cells

Stimulates a natural healing response, improving fluid outflow

Argon Laser Trabeculoplasty (ALT)

Older technique using higher-energy laser

Less commonly used since SLT offers similar benefits with fewer side effects

Office-based procedure (no operating room)

Quick (5–10 minutes), minimal discomfort

Can reduce or eliminate need for some eye drops

Repeatable (especially SLT)

IOP reduction may be modest (15–30%)

Effect may wear off over time (months to years)

Potential side effects: temporary pressure spike, mild inflammation

Who is a good candidate?Patients seeking to reduce daily medications, those with adherence issues or mild to moderate glaucoma not controlled by drops alone.

Surgical Procedures: When More Is Needed

If eye drops and laser therapy don't achieve target IOP, surgery may be recommended. Surgeries create a new drainage channel to lower eye pressure more effectively.

Trabeculectomy

Creates a small flap in the white of the eye (sclera) to drain fluid into a bleb

Often considered the "gold standard" for moderate to advanced glaucoma

Tube Shunt (Glaucoma Drainage Device)

Implants a small tube leading fluid to a plate under the conjunctiva

Useful in complex cases or when trabeculectomy isn't suitable

Minimally Invasive Glaucoma Surgery (MIGS)

Includes procedures like iStent, Hydrus microstent, Trabectome

Less tissue disruption, faster recovery

Typically offers modest IOP reduction—often combined with cataract surgery

Greater and more sustained IOP reduction

May significantly reduce or eliminate need for drops

Surgical risks: infection, bleeding, scarring, hypotony (too low pressure)

Recovery time and follow-up visits

Combining Treatments and Lifestyle Considerations

Glaucoma treatment often involves a stepwise, combined approach:

Start with one or two eye drops.

Add or switch medications if target IOP isn't met.

Introduce laser therapy to reduce medication burden.

Move to surgery if IOP remains elevated or optic nerve damage progresses.

Lifestyle tweaks can support treatment success:

Adhere strictly to drop schedules; set alarms or use drop reminders

Attend all follow-up visits; visual field and OCT tests monitor progression

Maintain a healthy diet, exercise safely and manage blood pressure

Avoid activities that spike eye pressure (e.g., certain yoga poses)

Conclusion: Working With Your Doctor

Choosing among glaucoma treatment options is a collaborative process. Each approach—eye drops, laser or surgery—has advantages and potential risks. Your doctor will tailor a plan based on your type of glaucoma, risk factors, lifestyle and treatment goals.
