The flu vaccine was shipped to clinics around the nation last week, and doctors recommend getting it in October or November, before the height of the flu season in January. But what about those stories about people getting sick from the shot?

The vaccine gets all kinds of a bad rap, according to Thomas Campbell, M.D., professor of family medicine at the University of Rochester. Many people think they’re getting injected with a weak strain of the virus, but the shot is a dead version, which stimulates an immune response and helps the body build protective antibodies to fight off sickness all winter.

After getting the shot you can expect to feel a sore shoulder or maybe a low-grade fever, but that’s about it, Campbell says. If you start to feel ill, chances are you’re not experiencing a side effect—you probably caught a cold.

“Many times when you get a flu shot, there are flu and cold viruses circulating,” Campbell says. “People think they got sick from the shot, but a vast majority of those are ill simply because they got a separate infection not related to the vaccine.”

Doctors are fairly certain they’ve identified the correct flu strain this season, unlike last year when two of the three vaccines prepared early in the year failed to treat the flu strains that actually emerged.

The shot is most often recommended for those over 50 or those who have a chronic illness such as diabetes or asthma, since complications from the flu could land them in the hospital.

People lose 2 to 5 days of work when they get influenza, Campbell says. So if you’re on the fence, go ahead and get the vaccine—that way you won’t end up losing income.
