Why Your Hip Won't Heal: PRP for Gluteal Tendinopathy & Medical Next Steps

Gluteal tendinopathy (commonly called "hip tendonitis" or "hip bursitis") can be stubborn and painful. When your hip doesn't heal as expected, daily activities and sleep suffer. Understanding why healing stalls and exploring advanced treatments—like PRP for gluteal tendinopathy—can get you back on track.

What Is Gluteal Tendinopathy?

Gluteal tendinopathy refers to degeneration or inflammation of the tendons that attach the gluteus medius and minimus muscles to the outer hip. These tendons stabilize your pelvis during walking, running, and climbing stairs.

Common symptoms:

Tenderness at the outer hip, often radiating down the thigh

Pain when lying on the affected side

Discomfort during activities like walking, standing on one leg, or climbing

Stiffness and weakness in the hip, especially after rest

When conservative measures stall or fail, you may wonder, "Why won't this heal?"

Why Your Hip Tendon Struggles to Heal

Poor Blood SupplyTendons have a limited blood flow compared to muscles. Reduced circulation means fewer nutrients and repair cells reach the damaged area.

Biomechanical Factors

Leg length differences

Flat feet or high arches

Hip or spine alignment issues

Muscle imbalances around the hip and pelvis

Overuse and Repetitive StressLong-distance running, cycling, and prolonged standing can overload the tendons beyond their capacity to repair.

Age and DegenerationTendon fibers become less elastic and more prone to micro-tears as we age. Natural wear and tear can outpace healing.

Systemic Health Issues

Diabetes or thyroid problems

Obesity

Smoking (reduces blood flow and delays tissue repair)

Certain medications (e.g., fluoroquinolone antibiotics)

Delayed or Inadequate TreatmentIgnoring early signs or relying solely on painkillers without addressing underlying issues (like muscle weakness or movement patterns) can prolong recovery.

What Is PRP and How Can It Help?

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) therapy harnesses your body's own healing potential. Here's a simple breakdown:

Blood Draw: A small amount of your blood is collected.

Centrifugation: The sample is spun to concentrate platelets—the cells rich in growth factors.

Injection: The platelet-rich layer is injected directly into the injured tendon under ultrasound guidance.

Is PRP Right for You?

You've tried rest, ice, anti-inflammatory medications, and physical therapy for at least 3–6 months without meaningful improvement.

You prefer to avoid or delay surgery.

Medical Next Steps Beyond PRP

While PRP can be a powerful tool, a multi-modal approach often yields the best results. Here's how to build a comprehensive plan:

1. Physical Therapy & Exercise

Strengthening: Focus on hip abductors, glutes, core stabilizers, and pelvic muscles.

Stretching: Gentle lengthening of hip flexors, hamstrings, and IT band.

Movement Retraining: Correct gait and posture to offload the injured tendon.

2. Shockwave Therapy

Uses acoustic waves to stimulate blood flow and healing.

[Stop the Pain PRP](https://glp-insights.xianfengchem.com/blog/7804120351.html)

May reduce pain when combined with exercises.

3. Orthotics and Bracing

Custom shoe inserts can correct foot mechanics and reduce hip stress.

A supportive belt or strap (e.g., trochanteric belt) can offload painful areas temporarily.

4. Medications

Short-term NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) for pain relief—use cautiously and under guidance to avoid affecting tendon healing.

Topical anti-inflammatories or patches applied near the hip.

5. Corticosteroid Injections

Can provide quick pain relief but may weaken tendon tissue if overused.

Best reserved for severe flare-ups unresponsive to other treatments.

6. Surgery

Considered when conservative measures (including PRP) fail after 6–12 months.

Tendon repair or debridement can remove degenerative tissue and reattach healthy fibers.

Lifestyle and Self-Care Tips

Activity Modification: Temporarily reduce high-impact exercises. Substitute with swimming or cycling.

Weight Management: Even small weight reductions can decrease hip loading.

When to Seek Further Evaluation

If you experience any of the following, seek medical attention promptly:

Sudden worsening of pain or onset of fever

Inability to bear weight on the leg

Significant leg swelling or redness around the hip

Numbness or tingling that extends below the knee
