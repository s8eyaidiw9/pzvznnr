Are genetics involved in alopecia?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Genetics play a major role in alopecia. Alopecia areata is highly polygenic, involving many immune and hair follicle genes, while androgenetic alopecia (male and female pattern baldness) is strongly heritable, with androgen-receptor gene variants being especially influential. A family history of hair loss increases your risk but does not guarantee you will experience it.

Genes don't act alone—they interact with hormones, nutrition, stress, illness, and medications to influence hair loss. Routine genetic testing is not standard practice; instead, diagnosis and treatment depend on the specific type of alopecia.

Because hair loss has many overlapping causes, identifying the underlying type matters for choosing the right treatment. Explanation

Are genetics involved in alopecia?

Alopecia—medical speak for hair loss—can affect anyone at any age. While factors such as hormones, stress and environment play roles, genetics are a key piece of the puzzle. Understanding "alopecia genetics" can help you and your doctor make sense of why hair may thin, fall out in patches, or stop growing altogether.

Genetic Foundations of Alopecia AreataAlopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune hair-loss condition in which your immune system mistakenly attacks hair follicles. Multiple studies, including a landmark 2010 genome-wide association study by Petukhova and Christiano (Nat Genet, 20336192), have shown AA is highly polygenic—meaning many genes contribute small amounts of risk. Key findings include:

Immune-related loci• HLA region variants (major histocompatibility complex)• ULBP3 and ULBP6 genes, which help immune cells recognize stressed hair-follicle cells

Hair-follicle integrity loci• Genes involved in hair-growth cycles and follicle structure

Shared autoimmunity signals• Overlap with genetic risk factors for rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes and other autoimmune diseases

What this means in everyday terms:

No single "alopecia areata gene" determines your fate—dozens to hundreds of small genetic tweaks add up to change your risk.

Genetic Insights into Androgenetic AlopeciaAndrogenetic alopecia (AGA), often called male-pattern or female-pattern baldness, is the most common form of hair loss. A 2001 genetic analysis by Ellis and Harrap (J Invest Dermatol, 11321928) highlighted a strong heritable component. Key genetic factors include:

Androgen receptor (AR) gene variants• Located on the X chromosome (men inherit it from their mother)• Increased sensitivity of hair follicles to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a testosterone byproduct

Non-AR loci identified in population studies• Chromosome 20p11 region variants• Other autosomal regions still under investigation

Real-world takeaways:

AGA often runs in families—if close relatives experienced thinning or balding, your risk is higher.

Both men and women can inherit these variants; however, female-pattern hair loss tends to present as diffuse thinning across the scalp, rather than a receding hairline or vertex balding.

Lifestyle and hormonal factors (e.g., menopause, weight changes, certain medications) interact with genetic predisposition to influence when and how much hair you lose.

Beyond AA and AGA: Other Forms of Alopecia GeneticsWhile AA and AGA have the strongest genetic evidence, other hair-loss types have more limited data:

Alopecia totalis/universalis (complete loss of scalp or body hair)• Often viewed as severe extensions of AA, sharing some genetic risk factors.

Cicatricial (scarring) alopecias• Genetic links are less clear and vary by subtype (e.g., lichen planopilaris, frontal fibrosing alopecia).

Telogen effluvium (shedding)• Primarily triggered by stressors (illness, surgery, thyroid dysfunction); genetic role is minor.

Clinical Implications: Testing and TreatmentAt present, routine genetic testing for alopecia is not standard practice. Instead, doctors rely on:

Detailed personal and family history

Scalp examination (dermoscopy/trichoscopy)

Blood tests to rule out underlying conditions (thyroid, iron studies, autoimmune markers)

Scalp biopsy in uncertain cases

Treatment approaches vary by alopecia type:

Alopecia Areata• Topical or intralesional corticosteroids to calm the immune attack• Topical immunotherapy (e.g., diphencyprone) in patchy AA• Emerging JAK inhibitor pills showing promise in clinical trials

Androgenetic Alopecia• Topical minoxidil to stimulate follicles• Oral finasteride (men only) to reduce DHT levels• Low-level laser therapy, platelet-rich plasma injections, hair transplant surgery

General supportive measures• Nutritional optimization (iron, biotin, vitamin D)• Gentle hair-care practices to minimize breakage

Key point: early intervention often yields better results. If you notice unexplained hair loss, don't wait for more hair to fall—seek medical advice.

When to See a DoctorWhile most forms of alopecia are not life-threatening, they can signal other health issues. See a doctor promptly if you experience:

Rapid, widespread hair loss (could indicate systemic illness)

Scalp pain, swelling or pus (possible infection)

Fatigue, weight changes or other systemic symptoms (rule out thyroid disease, anemia, autoimmune disorders)

Emotional distress from hair loss (mental-health support may help)

And always speak to a doctor about anything life-threatening or serious.
