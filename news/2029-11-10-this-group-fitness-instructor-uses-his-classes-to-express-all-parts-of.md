# This Group Fitness Instructor Uses His Classes to Express All Parts of Himself

This story is part of Black in Fitness, a series of articles highlighting the challenges and triumphs faced by Black trainers, athletes, and gym owners within the fitness community from their own perspective. Read the rest of the stories here.

Solidcore Senior Master Coach James Crawford Barnett, 34, champions the transformative power of mindfulness. His class mantra, "Strong Thoughts Equals Strong Actions," sets the tone for an intense Pilates-based workout that pushes participants to their limits. As an "out and proud" member of the LGBTQ+ community, he’s an advocate for bringing communities together and instilling confidence in everyone who steps foot into the studio.

[His Classes to](https://skinsequence.escortskhi.com/)

I STARTED FITNESS journey about 10 years ago. Growing up, I played a ton of sports. I was a diver all the way from middle school through college, where I was a Division I athlete. One thing that I loved about it was even though it's an individual sport, you train with a team. After graduating, I really missed that team environment. I missed that aspect of being able to work with other people, and have them push me and me push them. That's how I fell in love with group fitness.

[Parts of](https://cellular.wiki/d0k53yl/)

I found Solidcore [a Pilates-inspired group training gym] when I was 24 years old after trying all sorts of classes in Washington, D.C., and it’s a different level of intensity that closely matched what I was missing in diving. So I started going religiously. After five or six months, they approached me about teaching [classes]. Choosing to become an instructor (while still working full-time as a consultant) has taken both my life and fitness career to a whole different level.

When I first started teaching at Solidcore 2014, and really everywhere in the fitness space, it was just white bodies everywhere. It can feel a bit isolating to go into a new space and be the only Black person in the room. I feel like everyone's watching us, and they automatically have some kind of idea about who we are. Not that it's our job necessarily to change any of that, but we take that on sometimes, right? That can be difficult.

I’m out and proud, and it brings another level to my coaching. I can just be as wild and crazy on the mic as I want to be. There is this liberating feeling that I get when I teach because I am able to freely express all parts of me. The more authentic I am, the lighter I feel. I just live with more joy. I think that creates a space that feels safe for people, especially women.

However, being both Black and queer has certainly compounded the challenge of accessing opportunities and advancing in the fitness industry. It’s particularly challenging if you're working with a brand that does not base inclusion as a core value. If they’re just centering on your Blackness or queerness for a feature for Black History Month or Pride, it can feel performative.

I am also certified to teach meditation through Three Jewels studio, and the lessons I’ve learned through that have helped me accept myself and be more confident in spaces like this. You're really able to have these insights about anything from yourself to the world around you while you’re connecting with your breath. One of the things that I learned is that I don't have to prove my worth to anybody because I know that I have this inherent worth within me just by being a breathing body. With this mindset, I’m able to bring a lot of authenticity to my classes. Through my work, I really want to show people that they’re capable of pushing through physical and mental strain. There's a backdrop of mindfulness into everything that you're doing. It's not just about your body—you can really strengthen your mind while you're strengthening your body.

Something that I always try to keep in mind is I want the space [I teach in] to be inclusive. When I see someone of color in the space, I just try to give them the best experience because I know that there's a level of intimidation that people can have when they go into those spaces. The workout itself is already intimidating, and being the only minority in the room amplifies that. I do my best to alleviate those feelings because I’ve definitely been there.

[Fitness Instructor Uses](https://lumina-dermis.sunsimiaosd.com/blog/9952726778.html)

At Solidcore, we have different employee resource groups (ERGs). There’s one specific to people of color. Within that, we brainstorm different actions that can translate on national or regional levels. A few years ago, the group hosted a donation class that raised money for the Marsha P. Johnson Institute, which was great to be a part of.

More representation would definitely be helpful in making the industry more equitable—having more people of color teaching classes, managing, and owning studios. It really does make a difference—I've had other coaches tell me in the past, "You helped me want to work here because you work here." But the bigger thing is equity. Getting people of color to take classes and making these classes more affordable. The price point is a huge barrier. It makes fitness and wellness seem like something that's only for the elite, and that’s not true.

I’m definitely excited about the industry and where it's going. In terms of instructors, diversity is definitely increasing, especially in metropolitan areas. My hope is for people of color to get the same opportunities that our white co-workers get. Creating a personal brand and getting a following is one thing. But being able to get some of the really fun, exciting opportunities that come from that, would be awesome.
